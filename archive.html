Douglas v. Bhakta
Court of Appeals of Georgia, Second Division · decided Jun 22, 2026 · No. A26A0189 · affirmed in part, reversed in part, and remanded
autoprocedure

In an auto-accident suit, defendant admitted in his answer that he was a Kansas resident at the time of the accident and at the time of suit but did not assert insufficiency of service of process by motion filed before or simultaneously with the answer. The Court of Appeals held that a defendant waives the defense of insufficiency of service of process under OCGA section 9-11-12(h)(1)(B) by failing to raise it in the original answer or a contemporaneously filed motion, and that a general reference to lack of personal jurisdiction does not preserve the service defense. The court further held that, because defendant admitted out-of-state residency in his answer, he was subject to service under the Nonresident Motorist Act, and his later conclusory affidavit asserting Georgia residency could not overcome that binding judicial admission, which he never moved to withdraw. The court also clarified that a nonresident defendant involved in a Georgia accident is subject to jurisdiction under the NRMA when nonresident at both the time of accident and time of suit, while the Long Arm Statute applies when the defendant was a Georgia resident at the time of the accident and later became a nonresident. The dismissal was reversed.

Why it matters: It clarifies that the service-of-process defense in NRMA auto cases must be raised in the original answer or a simultaneously filed motion or it is waived, and that an answer admission of residency is a binding judicial admission that a later self-serving affidavit cannot overcome absent a motion to withdraw.

procedure

The trial court had granted defendant's motion to withdraw deemed admissions under OCGA section 9-11-36(b), including admissions concerning residence. Following its holding in Division 1, the Court of Appeals reversed the withdrawal as to the residency-related requests because defendant had admitted Kansas residency and failed to preserve the service defense, but found no abuse of discretion as to the remaining requests and directed that discovery be reopened on remand in light of newly provided eyewitness evidence.

Why it matters: It illustrates that withdrawal of deemed admissions under OCGA section 9-11-36(b) cannot be used to undo a binding pleading admission that controls a dispositive jurisdictional issue.

Yamaha Motor Manufacturing Corporation of America v. Hall
Court of Appeals of Georgia, Fourth Division · decided Jun 22, 2026 · No. A26A0228 · reversed
damagesprocedure

In a products-liability action arising from a golf cart rollover, the trial court excluded the specific contents of the manufacturer's warning labels and operator's manual, allowing only evidence that warnings existed and how they were delivered. The Court of Appeals held that the contents of the warnings were relevant and admissible to the risk-utility analysis on the design-defect claim under the Banks 'efficacy of warnings' factor, even though those contents were inadmissible on the communication-based failure-to-warn claim because the operator never read them. The court reasoned that evidence inadmissible for one purpose may be admissible for another and that the error was not harmless, and it rejected the contention that the manufacturer waived the argument by not using the phrase 'risk-utility analysis' below.

Why it matters: It clarifies that the contents of a manufacturer's warnings are admissible on the design-defect risk-utility analysis under Banks even when those contents are inadmissible on a failure-to-warn claim, and confirms that no magic words are required to preserve a relevance argument.

procedure

The manufacturer argued for the first time on appeal that it owed no duty to ensure its warning labels and operator's manual remained with the golf cart through the product's life cycle. The court held the argument was not preserved because, under OCGA section 9-11-50 (a), a directed-verdict motion must state its specific grounds, and the manufacturer never raised the absence of a duty when it moved for a directed verdict below, having instead argued only the adequacy of communication and proximate cause.

Why it matters: It reinforces that a ground for directed verdict not specifically stated in the trial court motion cannot be raised for the first time on appeal.

damages

The manufacturer invited the court to revisit AU Medical Center v. Dale, in which a plurality held that the term 'brought' in the former version of OCGA section 51-12-33 (b) refers to the number of named defendants remaining at the time of trial, which had led the trial court to enter the full judgment against the manufacturer despite the jury allocating fault to dismissed non-parties. The court declined the invitation to revisit that holding.

Why it matters: It signals that the court will not reconsider its apportionment construction in AU Medical Center v. Dale regarding allocation of fault to dismissed non-parties under the former statute.

T. M. v. University of Maryland Medical System Corporation
Supreme Court of the United States · decided Jun 18, 2026 · No. 25-197 · affirmed
procedure

The Supreme Court held that the Rooker-Feldman doctrine bars federal district court jurisdiction over cases brought by state-court losers complaining of injuries caused by state-court judgments rendered before the federal proceedings commenced and seeking district court review and rejection of those judgments, regardless of whether the state-court judgment remains subject to further review in state appellate proceedings. The Court rejected the argument that the doctrine applies only to final judgments rendered by the highest court of a State, reasoning that the doctrine rests on the functional distinction between original and appellate jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. sections 1331 and 1257, not solely on a negative inference from section 1257. Petitioner had sued in federal court to declare a state consent order unconstitutional and unenforceable while simultaneously appealing it in state court. The Court resolved a conflict among the Courts of Appeals.

Why it matters: The decision clarifies the scope of a threshold jurisdictional doctrine and confirms that Rooker-Feldman can bar a federal suit even when the underlying state-court judgment is still on appeal, which directly affects civil litigants pursuing parallel state and federal proceedings.

Lloyd v. City of Atlanta
Court of Appeals of Georgia, Third Division · decided Jun 16, 2026 · No. A26A0625 · affirmed
proceduredamages

Plaintiff, injured when his bike tire became lodged in a city storm drain grate, sent an ante litem notice jointly addressed to the City of Atlanta and Fulton County demanding a "global settlement amount" of $2,000,000. The Court of Appeals held that this notice failed to comply with OCGA section 36-33-5(e), which requires a specific amount of monetary damages being sought from the municipal corporation, because demanding a single global amount from two entities jointly does not identify the specific amount sought from the city alone and leaves the city to speculate what portion constituted an offer it could accept. The court noted it need not decide whether subsection (e) requires strict or substantial compliance because the notice failed under either standard, and rejected the argument that use of "and/or" cured the defect, finding the term was used only to reference the applicable notice statutes.

Why it matters: The decision clarifies that an ante litem notice presenting a single combined settlement demand against a municipality and another governmental entity, without specifying the amount sought from the municipality alone, fails the specific-amount requirement of OCGA section 36-33-5(e) and bars suit as a condition precedent.

Cayamcela v. Advocacy Trust, LLC
Supreme Court of Georgia · decided Jun 16, 2026 · Nos. S26A0229 & S26A0242 · affirmed
expertdamagesprocedure

In a medical malpractice and wrongful death action arising from a patient's death in the ICU after childbirth, the jury returned a $42 million verdict against a doctor and a medical staffing agency. The Supreme Court of Georgia affirmed the trial court's exclusion under OCGA section 24-7-702(b) of a defense expert's testimony about ICU care, holding the court acted within its gatekeeping discretion where the expert repeatedly admitted he had not reviewed the relevant ICU records well enough to opine on that care and that the matter fell outside his specialty. The court rejected the argument that these gaps went only to credibility rather than admissibility.

Why it matters: The decision confirms that a trial court may exclude expert testimony as unreliable under Rule 702(b) when the expert concedes an insufficient factual basis, and that such deficiencies bear on admissibility, not merely weight.

damages

The court held that the noneconomic damages cap of OCGA section 51-13-1(b) could not be constitutionally applied to the jury's verdict. Relying on its companion decision in Clark v. Leigh, issued the same day, the court reaffirmed that stare decisis preserves Nestlehutt and that where any part of a medical malpractice award is for noneconomic damages subject to the right to a jury trial, the cap cannot be applied without violating that right.

Why it matters: The ruling confirms that the medical malpractice noneconomic damages cap remains unenforceable as applied to jury verdicts that include noneconomic damages, leaving Nestlehutt intact and extending its reasoning to wrongful death awards.

procedure

The court held that the defendants affirmatively waived their challenge to a jury instruction stating that defendants bore the burden of proof on whether other medical professionals were the sole proximate cause. By proposing the opposite instruction, then withdrawing all proposed instructions, and stating multiple times that they had no objection to the court's charge, the defendants waived even substantial-error review under OCGA section 5-5-24(c).

Why it matters: The decision clarifies that affirmative waiver, by withdrawing requested instructions and acquiescing in the charge given, forecloses substantial-error review of an instructional error on appeal.

proceduredamages

The court affirmed the roughly $11.8 million attorney fee award under OCGA section 9-11-68, holding the plaintiffs' settlement offer satisfied subsection (a)(7) because it stated plaintiffs would pay their own fees and expenses, which were incurred for the claims rather than part of them. The court further held that OCGA section 9-15-16 and Couch bar use of a contingency fee agreement to prove the reasonableness of fees but do not bar considering the agreement for other purposes, such as proving total fees incurred, and that the trial court properly established reasonableness through other evidence.

Why it matters: The decision clarifies that a contingency fee agreement may be used to establish total fees incurred even though it cannot prove their reasonableness under OCGA section 9-15-16, and addresses what satisfies the offer requirements of OCGA section 9-11-68(a)(7).

Clark v. Leigh
Supreme Court of Georgia · decided Jun 16, 2026 · Nos. S26A0349 & S26X0350 · vacated and remanded with direction
damagesproceduretort reform

In a medical malpractice and wrongful death action where a jury awarded $29,250,000 for the full value of the decedent's life, $2,500,000 for pain and suffering, and medical expenses, the trial court applied the OCGA section 51-13-1(b) noneconomic damages cap to reduce the wrongful death award to $350,000. The Supreme Court of Georgia reaffirmed Nestlehutt, holding that the right to trial by jury under the Georgia Constitution does not permit applying the cap to pain and suffering and loss of consortium damages in medical malpractice actions, and declined to overrule it under stare decisis. The court then held that, under ordinary principles of statutory construction and in light of Nestlehutt, the cap statute cannot operate at all on a judgment in a case like this one, where the jury's verdict includes noneconomic damages for a cause of action to which the jury trial right applies, because making it operative would require impermissibly rewriting the statute. The court vacated the order applying the cap and remanded for the trial court to decide the defendants' excessiveness claim as to the wrongful death award.

Why it matters: The decision reaffirms that the OCGA section 51-13-1(b) medical malpractice noneconomic damages cap is unenforceable as to constitutionally protected jury-determined damages and extends that result, on statutory-construction grounds, to render the cap inoperative on the entire judgment including 'full value of life' wrongful death damages in such cases.

proceduredamages

The plaintiffs argued the defendants waived the right to assert the statutory damages cap by failing to raise it in the pretrial order. The court held the trial court did not abuse its discretion in allowing the defendants to raise the cap for the first time in their post-trial motions, reasoning that the cap statute is not an affirmative defense but a statutory limit on the amount of recoverable damages that is triggered only once a verdict exceeds the threshold.

Why it matters: It clarifies that a statutory damages cap need not be raised in the pretrial order or at trial and may be asserted for the first time in a timely post-trial motion.

Crook v. Six Flags Over Georgia II, L.P.
Court of Appeals of Georgia, Third Division · decided Jun 15, 2026 · No. A26A0470 · reversed and remanded with direction
procedure

Plaintiff, the surviving spouse and administrator of an employee who died after falling from a cargo van at a theme park, sued the employer and a co-worker, and the trial court dismissed under OCGA 9-11-12(b)(1) for lack of subject matter jurisdiction based on the exclusive remedy provision of the Workers' Compensation Act. The Court of Appeals held that the Act's exclusivity bar is a statutory affirmative defense, not a question of subject matter jurisdiction, so the employer bears the burden of proving the bar applies and the defense can be waived if not timely raised. The court disapproved Kellogg Co. v. Pinkston, Savannah Hospitality Servs. v. Scriven, and T & R S. Sanitation v. Lowe to the extent they treated the exclusivity bar as jurisdictional. The trial court therefore erred in placing the burden on plaintiff to disprove the bar.

Why it matters: This decision establishes that the Workers' Compensation Act exclusive remedy provision must be litigated as a waivable affirmative defense, with the burden on the employer, rather than as a non-waivable subject matter jurisdiction bar, which changes how WCA exclusivity is raised and proved in Georgia tort and wrongful death cases.

Allied Property & Casualty Insurance Company v. Bloodworth Wholesale Drugs Inc.
U.S. Court of Appeals for the Eleventh Circuit · decided Jun 11, 2026 · Nos. 24-11398 & 25-11185 · question certified · unpublished, not binding precedent
coveragefirst impression

Two pharmacies sued for their alleged role in the opioid epidemic sought defense and indemnity under liability and druggist liability policies covering damages 'because of bodily injury.' The insurers contended that the underlying government and healthcare suits seek economic losses not tied to identifiable injured persons and are therefore not covered, and the district courts granted summary judgment for the insurers. The Eleventh Circuit found no clear controlling Georgia or Florida precedent on whether such opioid lawsuits seek damages 'because of bodily injury,' distinguishing Georgia decisions like Cesnik, Lunceford, Presidential Hotel, and O'Dell as not dispositive. It certified to the Supreme Court of Georgia and the Supreme Court of Florida the question whether the policies require the insurers to defend and indemnify the insureds in the underlying opioid litigation.

Why it matters: The certification flags an unresolved, high-value coverage question of whether CGL and druggist liability policies covering 'damages because of bodily injury' reach opioid-epidemic suits seeking economic losses, leaving Georgia coverage law on the point to be settled by the Supreme Court of Georgia.

Waddle Trucking of Mississippi, Inc. v. Johnson
Court of Appeals of Georgia, Fourth Division · decided Jun 11, 2026 · No. A26A0145 · reversed
procedureauto

A trucking defendant in a personal injury action was served in state court, after which a codefendant removed the case to federal court, where the defendant filed an answer that was untimely under Fed.R.Civ.P. 81(c)(2). After remand, the state court entered default judgment against the defendant. The Court of Appeals held that the answer filed in district court, even though untimely there, prevented default in state court because the district court never struck the answer and no default was entered under Fed.R.Civ.P. 55, and because on remand the defendant still had time remaining to answer.

Why it matters: The decision clarifies that an answer filed in federal court after removal continues to have effect on remand and prevents entry of default in state court so long as the federal court did not strike it or enter default, even if the federal answer was untimely.

Keathley v. Buddy Ayers Construction, Inc.
Supreme Court of the United States · decided Jun 11, 2026 · No. 25-6 · vacated and remanded
procedureauto

A debtor in an open Chapter 13 bankruptcy failed to disclose a personal-injury claim arising from a car accident, and the lower courts applied judicial estoppel to bar the tort suit using the Fifth Circuit's two-factor test, asking only whether the debtor knew the underlying facts and had a potential motive to conceal. The Supreme Court, assuming without deciding that judicial estoppel can apply in the bankruptcy context and that inadvertence or mistake can be an exception, held that courts must look to the totality of the circumstances surrounding the omission. The Court found the Fifth Circuit's approach both too rigid, because equity eschews mechanical rules, and too broad, because its motive criterion is almost always satisfied, making it a poor fit for assessing whether an omission was actually inadvertent or mistaken.

Why it matters: It establishes that whether a plaintiff-debtor's failure to schedule a personal-injury claim was inadvertent or mistaken for judicial-estoppel purposes turns on the totality of the circumstances rather than a knowledge-plus-motive test, governing how civil litigants raise and defend against judicial estoppel when a tort claim was omitted from bankruptcy schedules.

Hodge v. Molson Coors Beverage Company USA, LLC
U.S. Court of Appeals for the Eleventh Circuit · decided Jun 11, 2026 · No. 25-12983 · vacated in part, affirmed in part, and remanded · unpublished, not binding precedent
auto

A family injured by a semitruck sued a freight broker and a shipper, alleging negligent hiring of the carrier. The district court held that the FAAAA preempted the negligent-selection claim against the broker. Following the Supreme Court's decision in Montgomery v. Caribe Transport II, which abrogated the Eleventh Circuit's prior holding in Aspen American Insurance Co. v. Landstar Ranger, the court vacated and remanded, noting the district court had relied on now-abrogated caselaw.

Why it matters: It applies the Supreme Court's recent holding that the FAAAA does not preempt negligent-selection-of-carrier claims against brokers, abrogating prior Eleventh Circuit precedent that had foreclosed such claims.

auto

The court affirmed dismissal of the negligent-hiring claim against the shipper, applying Maryland law, which requires that the defendant's failure to undertake reasonable inquiry resulted in the contractor's hiring and that the hiring proximately caused the plaintiff's injury. The shipper hired Schneider, who hired Coyote, who hired the carrier, leaving the shipper three steps removed from hiring the operator. Because plaintiffs neither identified authority extending the theory that far nor alleged that the selection of Schneider was negligent, the claim failed, and influence over the hiring process was held not equivalent to actually hiring the carrier.

Why it matters: It clarifies that negligent-hiring liability under Maryland law does not extend to a party several contractual layers removed from the entity that hired the operator absent an allegation tying the harm to the directly hired contractor.

Ya Mon Expeditions, LLC v. YATCO, LLC
U.S. Court of Appeals for the Eleventh Circuit · decided Jun 10, 2026 · No. 25-10140 · affirmed · unpublished, not binding precedent
procedure

In an interlocutory appeal from the denial of a motion to compel arbitration under the FAA, an MLS company sought to compel nonsignatory yacht-seller plaintiffs to arbitrate antitrust claims based on a subscription agreement their brokers allegedly accepted. The court held that under Florida law, which governs the threshold question of whether an arbitration agreement exists, the party seeking to compel arbitration bears the burden of proving an enforceable agreement, and the movant relied on speculation and assumption rather than evidence that the brokers actually listed the plaintiffs' yachts on its website or accepted the relevant version of the agreement. The court rejected the movant's argument that the nonsignatory plaintiffs opposing arbitration bore the burden of disproving its evidence.

Why it matters: It clarifies that the FAA presumption favoring arbitration does not apply to the threshold question of whether an agreement to arbitrate exists, and that state law places the burden of proving an enforceable arbitration agreement on the party seeking to compel it.

procedure

The court held that the nonsignatory plaintiffs could not be bound to arbitrate under an agency-ratification theory. Applying the Florida Supreme Court's full-knowledge requirement, the court held that a principal ratifies an agent's pre-agency acceptance of a contract only on a showing that the principal had full knowledge of the agreement and its terms, and constructive knowledge does not suffice. Because the movant submitted no evidence that the plaintiffs knew of the agreement or its terms, and the brokers' knowledge could not be imputed because it was acquired before any agency relationship formed, ratification did not apply.

Why it matters: It establishes that under controlling Florida Supreme Court precedent a nonsignatory principal cannot ratify an agent's pre-agency acceptance of an arbitration agreement absent full actual knowledge of the agreement and its terms.

procedure

The court held that equitable estoppel did not bind the nonsignatory plaintiffs to arbitrate. A signatory may enforce an arbitration clause against a nonsignatory under equitable estoppel only if the nonsignatory relies on the agreement to assert its claims and the dispute falls within the clause. Because the plaintiffs' federal antitrust claims arose from statutory duties unrelated to the subscription agreement and did not require reference to or construction of that agreement, the plaintiffs were not claiming the benefits of the contract while avoiding its burdens.

Why it matters: It clarifies that equitable estoppel cannot compel a nonsignatory to arbitrate where the asserted claims rest on independent statutory duties and do not depend on the contract containing the arbitration clause.

Vu v. City of Atlanta
Court of Appeals of Georgia, Third Division · decided Jun 9, 2026 · No. A26A0563 · reversed
autoprocedure

A city employee driving a city-owned truck rear-ended plaintiff's car, and plaintiff served a municipal ante litem notice demanding $1,000,000. The trial court dismissed the suit, accepting the city's argument that the notice failed to state a 'specific amount' under OCGA § 36-33-5(e) because it exceeded the $500,000 sovereign-immunity waiver for negligent motor-vehicle use. The Court of Appeals reversed, holding that OCGA § 36-92-2 does not cap or limit the waiver but automatically waives immunity up to $500,000 while specifying ways to increase it, and reaffirming that the demanded amount need not be one the municipality is capable of accepting.

Why it matters: The decision clarifies that a municipal ante litem notice satisfies the specific-amount requirement of OCGA § 36-33-5(e) even when the demand exceeds the automatic sovereign-immunity waiver under OCGA § 36-92-2.

GoAuto Insurance Company v. 2B Claims Services Inc.
U.S. Court of Appeals for the Eleventh Circuit · decided Jun 8, 2026 · No. 24-13643 · affirmed · unpublished, not binding precedent
bad faithauto

An insurer sued the third-party claims adjuster it hired, alleging the adjuster negligently failed to advise it about the risk of excess bad-faith liability after the insurer let a checks-in-hand policy-limits deadline pass and later settled for about $220,000 above the policy limits. Applying Florida bad-faith law, the Eleventh Circuit affirmed summary judgment for the adjuster, holding that a claims adjuster has no duty to advise an insurer about its own legal risk of excess bad-faith exposure because such advice constitutes legal advice, and that the insurer's own decision to demand signed releases and not deliver the checks by the deadline, not the adjuster's conduct, proximately caused the excess settlement. The court noted the insurer knew of the checks-in-hand requirement eleven days before the deadline and could have delivered the checks.

Why it matters: The decision clarifies that a third-party claims adjuster owes no duty to advise an insurer about its own extracontractual bad-faith exposure and that an insurer's independent settlement and payment decisions, not the adjuster's handling, control causation in a negligence claim against the adjuster.

State Farm Mut. Auto. Ins. Co. & GEICO Indemnity Co. v. Barnor-Cooper
Court of Appeals of Georgia, Fourth Division · decided May 20, 2026 · Nos. A26A0526 & A26A0550 · reversed
coverageauto

The Court of Appeals reversed the denial of summary judgment to State Farm and GEICO in a UM claim arising from a hit-and-run collision, holding the plaintiff was not entitled to UM benefits under either policy. Injured while making a delivery in a Turo-rented Buick, she was not an insured under the State Farm policy: the Buick was not a "temporary substitute car" because the scheduled vehicle it would have replaced had been sold, and the named-insured LLC is a separate legal entity from its members and managers.

procedureexpert

Montgomery v. Caribe Transport II, LLC
Supreme Court of the United States · decided May 14, 2026 · No. 24-1238 · reversed and remanded
auto

An injured motorist sued a transportation broker for negligent hiring, alleging that the broker selected a motor carrier with a subpar federal safety rating that was reasonably likely to cause crashes. The Court held that even assuming the FAAAA's preemption provision in 49 U.S.C. section 14501(c)(1) would otherwise reach the negligent-hiring claim, the safety exception in section 14501(c)(2)(A) saves it because such a claim is one regulating safety 'with respect to motor vehicles.' Construing 'with respect to' to mean 'concerns' or 'regards,' the Court reasoned that requiring a broker to exercise ordinary care in selecting a carrier concerns the trucks that will transport the goods.

Why it matters: The decision resolves a circuit split and establishes that state-law negligent-hiring claims against freight brokers who select unsafe motor carriers are not preempted by the FAAAA, abrogating the contrary Eleventh Circuit rule that had governed in Georgia.

Giles v. Greenhouse Apartments, LLC
Court of Appeals of Georgia, Fourth Division · decided Feb 6, 2026 · No. A25A2143 · affirmed
negligent securitypremises

The Court of Appeals affirmed summary judgment for an apartment owner and its manager in a negligent-security action by a tenant beaten by unknown assailants on the property in 2020. Mejia v. SK Battery America, Inc.
Court of Appeals of Georgia, Second Division · decided Jan 27, 2026 · No. A25A2164 · affirmed
premises

Harris v. Mahone
Court of Appeals of Georgia, Fifth Division · decided Mar 1, 2017 · No. A16A1748 · affirmed in part, reversed in part, and remanded with direction

In an auto negligence case, plaintiff sought attorney fees under OCGA Section 9-11-68 after recovering a verdict exceeding 125 percent of his pretrial counteroffer. The court held that a counteroffer made within 20 days of trial under subsection (a) must still remain open 30 days under subsection (c), and that fees under subsection (b)(2) run only from the date the offer is deemed rejected through entry of judgment. Because the counteroffer was deemed rejected three days after the trial concluded and plaintiff showed no fees incurred in the window between that date and judgment, no fees were recoverable; a counteroffer can avoid this result only by being withdrawn in writing before its expiration. Scapa Dryer Fabrics, Inc. v. Knight
Supreme Court of Georgia · decided Jul 5, 2016 · No. S15G1278 · reversed
expertdamages

In an asbestos mesothelioma case, the Supreme Court of Georgia held that an expert's cumulative exposure opinion, which told the jury that any exposure above background, even de minimis, was a contributing cause of the disease, did not 'fit' the legal causation standard and was inadmissible under former OCGA section 24-9-67.1 (b). The Court reaffirmed that, while a plaintiff need not prove a substantial contribution, a de minimis contribution does not satisfy Georgia's 'contributing factor' causation standard, and an expert must tie a cumulative exposure opinion to a reliable estimate of actual exposure showing more than de minimis exposure. Because the expert did not condition his causation opinion on such an estimate, its admission was an abuse of the trial court's gatekeeping discretion. The Court reversed the Court of Appeals' affirmance of the judgment.

Why it matters: The decision clarifies that expert causation testimony in toxic tort cases must satisfy the 'fit' requirement by being tied to a reliable estimate of more than de minimis exposure, and that an 'any exposure' or pure cumulative exposure theory unconnected to actual exposure is inadmissible.

Phillips v. Harmon

In a medical malpractice action, the Supreme Court of Georgia clarified that a defendant's duty to preserve evidence is triggered not only by actual or express notice of a claim from the plaintiff but whenever litigation is reasonably foreseeable to the party in control of the evidence, and that constructive notice may be shown by circumstances including the type and extent of injury, the clarity of fault, potential financial exposure, the frequency of litigation in similar circumstances, and the defendant's own conduct such as internal investigations and notification of counsel and insurers. The Court separately affirmed that the trial judge's ex parte communication with the deadlocked jury, conducted outside the presence of the parties and counsel and never disclosed before the verdict, violated the civil parties' right to be present and, under the unique circumstances including the inability to reconstruct the note, constituted harmful error requiring a new trial.

Why it matters: The decision reframes the trigger for a defendant's evidence-preservation duty around a reasonable-foreseeability standard with enumerated factors, disapproving prior precedent that required actual notice, and confirms that substantive ex parte judge-jury communications in civil trials can require a new trial.
