Ya Mon Expeditions, LLC v. YATCO, LLC
U.S. Court of Appeals for the Eleventh Circuit · decided Jun 10, 2026 · No. 25-10140 · affirmed · unpublished, not binding precedent
procedure
procedure

In an interlocutory appeal from the denial of a motion to compel arbitration under the FAA, an MLS company sought to compel nonsignatory yacht-seller plaintiffs to arbitrate antitrust claims based on a subscription agreement their brokers allegedly accepted. The court held that under Florida law, which governs the threshold question of whether an arbitration agreement exists, the party seeking to compel arbitration bears the burden of proving an enforceable agreement, and the movant relied on speculation and assumption rather than evidence that the brokers actually listed the plaintiffs' yachts on its website or accepted the relevant version of the agreement. The court rejected the movant's argument that the nonsignatory plaintiffs opposing arbitration bore the burden of disproving its evidence.

Why it matters: It clarifies that the FAA presumption favoring arbitration does not apply to the threshold question of whether an agreement to arbitrate exists, and that state law places the burden of proving an enforceable arbitration agreement on the party seeking to compel it.

procedure

The court held that the nonsignatory plaintiffs could not be bound to arbitrate under an agency-ratification theory. Applying the Florida Supreme Court's full-knowledge requirement, the court held that a principal ratifies an agent's pre-agency acceptance of a contract only on a showing that the principal had full knowledge of the agreement and its terms, and constructive knowledge does not suffice. Because the movant submitted no evidence that the plaintiffs knew of the agreement or its terms, and the brokers' knowledge could not be imputed because it was acquired before any agency relationship formed, ratification did not apply.

Why it matters: It establishes that under controlling Florida Supreme Court precedent a nonsignatory principal cannot ratify an agent's pre-agency acceptance of an arbitration agreement absent full actual knowledge of the agreement and its terms.

procedure

The court held that equitable estoppel did not bind the nonsignatory plaintiffs to arbitrate. A signatory may enforce an arbitration clause against a nonsignatory under equitable estoppel only if the nonsignatory relies on the agreement to assert its claims and the dispute falls within the clause. Because the plaintiffs' federal antitrust claims arose from statutory duties unrelated to the subscription agreement and did not require reference to or construction of that agreement, the plaintiffs were not claiming the benefits of the contract while avoiding its burdens.

Why it matters: It clarifies that equitable estoppel cannot compel a nonsignatory to arbitrate where the asserted claims rest on independent statutory duties and do not depend on the contract containing the arbitration clause.

Vu v. City of Atlanta
Court of Appeals of Georgia, Third Division · decided Jun 9, 2026 · No. A26A0563 · reversed
autoprocedure

A city employee driving a city-owned truck rear-ended plaintiff's car, and plaintiff served a municipal ante litem notice demanding $1,000,000. The trial court dismissed the suit, accepting the city's argument that the notice failed to state a 'specific amount' under OCGA § 36-33-5(e) because it exceeded the $500,000 sovereign-immunity waiver for negligent motor-vehicle use. The Court of Appeals reversed, holding that OCGA § 36-92-2 does not cap or limit the waiver but automatically waives immunity up to $500,000 while specifying ways to increase it, and reaffirming that the demanded amount need not be one the municipality is capable of accepting.

Why it matters: The decision clarifies that a municipal ante litem notice satisfies the specific-amount requirement of OCGA § 36-33-5(e) even when the demand exceeds the automatic sovereign-immunity waiver under OCGA § 36-92-2.

GoAuto Insurance Company v. 2B Claims Services Inc.
U.S. Court of Appeals for the Eleventh Circuit · decided Jun 8, 2026 · No. 24-13643 · affirmed · unpublished, not binding precedent
bad faithauto

An insurer sued the third-party claims adjuster it hired, alleging the adjuster negligently failed to advise it about the risk of excess bad-faith liability after the insurer let a checks-in-hand policy-limits deadline pass and later settled for about $220,000 above the policy limits. Applying Florida bad-faith law, the Eleventh Circuit affirmed summary judgment for the adjuster, holding that a claims adjuster has no duty to advise an insurer about its own legal risk of excess bad-faith exposure because such advice constitutes legal advice, and that the insurer's own decision to demand signed releases and not deliver the checks by the deadline, not the adjuster's conduct, proximately caused the excess settlement. The court noted the insurer knew of the checks-in-hand requirement eleven days before the deadline and could have delivered the checks.

Why it matters: The decision clarifies that a third-party claims adjuster owes no duty to advise an insurer about its own extracontractual bad-faith exposure and that an insurer's independent settlement and payment decisions, not the adjuster's handling, control causation in a negligence claim against the adjuster.

State Farm Mut. Auto. Ins. Co. & GEICO Indemnity Co. v. Barnor-Cooper
Court of Appeals of Georgia, Fourth Division · decided May 20, 2026 · Nos. A26A0526 & A26A0550 · reversed
coverageauto

The Court of Appeals reversed the denial of summary judgment to State Farm and GEICO in a UM claim arising from a hit-and-run collision, holding the plaintiff was not entitled to UM benefits under either policy. Injured while making a delivery in a Turo-rented Buick, she was not an insured under the State Farm policy: the Buick was not a "temporary substitute car" because the scheduled vehicle it would have replaced had been sold, and the named-insured LLC is a separate legal entity from its members and managers.

Why it matters: Reads the 'temporary substitute car' and named-insured provisions strictly: a borrowed vehicle is not a temporary substitute once the scheduled car it would replace has been sold, and an LLC policyholder is a separate legal entity from its members and managers, which narrows who qualifies as an insured for UM purposes.

Johnson v. Everett
Court of Appeals of Georgia, Fourth Division · decided May 20, 2026 · No. A26A0099 · vacated and remanded in part
procedureexpert

In a medical-malpractice case that ended in a defense verdict, the Court of Appeals held that the trial court mistakenly believed it had no choice but to let the jury consider the emergency-medical-care gross-negligence standard of O.C.G.A. § 51-1-29.5, an issue the parties had not listed in their pretrial order, when in fact the court had discretion to disallow it. The court vacated the denial of a new trial and remanded for the court to exercise that discretion, and found no error in admitting the defense expert's opinions or a complaint from another case.

Why it matters: A pretrial order frames the issues for trial, but the court keeps discretion over an issue the parties left out of it, and treating that discretion as nonexistent is reversible error.

Loveless v. GSH Investments, LLC
Court of Appeals of Georgia, First Division · decided Mar 26, 2026 · No. A26A0113 · affirmed in part, reversed in part
premises

The Court of Appeals reversed summary judgment for an apartment owner whose tenant's visitor was injured when an exterior wooden staircase collapsed, and affirmed the denial of the plaintiff's motions to compel. Before buying the complex the owner received an appraisal and an insurer survey flagging below-average condition and deferred maintenance, yet it never inspected the building exteriors or the stairways and kept no formal inspection process, relying on tenants to report problems. That evidence created a jury question on whether the owner had constructive knowledge of the hazard.

Why it matters: Prior reports flagging below-average condition, combined with the owner's lack of any inspection routine, can supply constructive knowledge of a hazard and create a jury question that defeats summary judgment.

Cannon v. Safeco Insurance Co. of Illinois
Court of Appeals of Georgia, First Division · decided Mar 16, 2026 · No. A25A1847 · reversed
coveragebad faithauto

The Court of Appeals reversed the dismissal of a failure-to-settle action against a liability insurer. After a $1.65 million excess judgment was entered against its insured following a fatal collision, the insured alleged that Safeco had interpleaded and tendered its $100,000 limits into the court registry without obtaining any release of the claims against him. Because an insurer must give equal consideration to the insured's interests and may be liable for an excess judgment for a negligent or bad-faith failure to settle, the complaint stated a claim.

Why it matters: Tendering policy limits by interpleader, without obtaining a release for the insured, does not by itself discharge the insurer or bar a later failure-to-settle or bad-faith claim.

Giles v. Greenhouse Apartments, LLC
Court of Appeals of Georgia, Fourth Division · decided Feb 6, 2026 · No. A25A2143 · affirmed
negligent securitypremises

The Court of Appeals affirmed summary judgment for an apartment owner and its manager in a negligent-security action by a tenant who was beaten by unknown assailants on the property. Mejia v. SK Battery America, Inc.
Court of Appeals of Georgia, Second Division · decided Jan 27, 2026 · No. A25A2164 · affirmed
premises

In a wrongful-death action arising from a fatal fall at a battery-plant construction site, the Court of Appeals affirmed summary judgment for the premises defendants on assumption of the risk. The decedent, a foreman repeatedly trained on and warned about the full tie-off rule, stepped onto a red-taped, non-weight-bearing louver without tying off and fell more than fifty feet, so no jury question remained on whether he knowingly and voluntarily encountered an obvious danger under O.C.G.A. § 51-3-1.

Why it matters: Repeated, documented safety training and the worker's own written hazard acknowledgment can establish assumption of the risk as a matter of law under O.C.G.A. § 51-3-1, even in a catastrophic-injury case.

SMG Construction Services, LLC v. Cook
Supreme Court of Georgia · decided Oct 15, 2025 · No. S25G0389 · vacated and remanded
premises

The Supreme Court of Georgia vacated a Court of Appeals decision that had reversed summary judgment for a property owner whose independent contractor fell from an exposed second-story ledge. The Court held the Court of Appeals conflated actual and constructive knowledge and wrongly used constructive-knowledge principles to create a fact question about the contractor's actual knowledge of the open, unguarded ledge. Because the lower court stopped there, it had not addressed all elements of the owner's defenses, so the case was remanded.

Why it matters: Actual knowledge of an open and obvious hazard is analyzed on its own terms under O.C.G.A. § 51-3-1 and is not blurred into the constructive-knowledge inquiry; this is the decision the Mejia panel applied.

Walmart Stores East, LP v. Leverette
Supreme Court of Georgia · decided Jun 24, 2025 · No. S24G1104 · vacated and remanded
damagespremises

The Supreme Court of Georgia vacated a $1 million "nominal damages" award in a premises injury case in which a shopper was struck by a heavy box. The Court held that nominal damages under Georgia law remain only a trivial sum, as at common law, and overruled the Court of Appeals precedent treating nominal damages as having no maximum. Because a million dollars is not a trivial sum, the judgment was vacated, with the remedy and invited-error questions left for the courts below.

Why it matters: Nominal damages under Georgia law remain only a trivial sum, and the Court overruled precedent that had treated them as having no ceiling, so a seven-figure 'nominal' award cannot stand.

Miller v. Golden Peanut Company, LLC
Supreme Court of Georgia · decided Aug 21, 2023 · No. S22G0905 · vacated and remanded with direction
expertauto

In a wrongful death action arising from a collision between a tractor-trailer and a car, the Supreme Court of Georgia held that the pre-2013 investigating officer rule, under which a law enforcement officer was treated as presumptively qualified as an expert on the cause of a collision, did not survive adoption of the current Evidence Code. Because OCGA section 24-7-702 contains no carve-out for law enforcement officers, a trial court must perform the same three-prong Daubert gatekeeping inquiry, qualification, reliability, and helpfulness, for expert opinion testimony from an investigating officer as for any other expert. The court found that the disputed accident-reconstruction opinions, including that the deceased driver was distracted, constituted expert rather than lay testimony, and that the trial court abused its discretion by assessing only qualification and not reliability or helpfulness. The court vacated the Court of Appeals' opinion, directed it to vacate the trial court's order, and remanded, and expressly disapproved Court of Appeals decisions applying the investigating officer rule to expert testimony in civil cases.

Why it matters: The decision establishes that investigating officers who offer accident-reconstruction or causation opinions in civil cases are subject to the full Daubert reliability and helpfulness analysis, eliminating the prior presumption of expert qualification.

Aspen American Insurance Company v. Landstar Ranger, Inc.
U.S. Court of Appeals for the Eleventh Circuit · decided Apr 13, 2023 · No. 22-10740 · affirmed
procedure

After a thief posing as a registered carrier stole a shipment, the cargo owner's insurer sued the transportation broker for negligent selection of the carrier, and the district court dismissed the claims as preempted by the FAAAA. The Eleventh Circuit affirmed, holding that the FAAAA's express preemption provision bars state-law negligence claims against a broker based on carrier selection because such claims relate to a broker's service with respect to the transportation of property. The court further held that the FAAAA's safety exception does not save the claims because a broker's carrier-selection services have only an indirect, not a direct, connection to motor vehicles, so the claims are not 'with respect to motor vehicles.'

Why it matters: This decision establishes binding Eleventh Circuit authority that the FAAAA preempts state-law negligent-carrier-selection claims against freight brokers and that the safety exception does not apply to such broker claims, defining the scope of broker liability in trucking cases in the circuit.

Adventure Motorsports Reinsurance, Ltd. v. Interstate National Dealer Services, Inc.
Supreme Court of Georgia · decided Dec 14, 2021 · Nos. S21G0008 & S21G0015 · reversed and remanded in part, vacated and remanded in part
procedure

The Supreme Court of Georgia held that vacatur of an arbitration award for manifest disregard of the law under OCGA section 9-9-13 (b) (5) requires concrete evidence that the arbitrator was conscious of the correct law and intentionally and knowingly chose to ignore it, and that a mere erroneous interpretation or misapplication of law to the facts does not suffice. Applying this standard, the court concluded that the arbitrator interpreted the parties' contracts and fashioned an equitable remedy that drew its essence from the agreements, and that nothing in the record showed a deliberate decision to disregard applicable law. The court reversed the Court of Appeals' decision vacating the confirmation of the award and remanded for consideration of the unresolved argument under OCGA section 9-9-13 (b) (3) that the arbitrator overstepped his authority, and it vacated and remanded the related appeal concerning a delayed-payment penalty as potentially no longer moot.

Why it matters: The decision clarifies the demanding evidentiary threshold for vacating arbitration awards on manifest-disregard grounds under Georgia's Arbitration Code, an issue that recurs in disputes governed by arbitration clauses.

2020

Quynn v. Hulsey
Supreme Court of Georgia · decided Nov 2, 2020 · No. S19G1612 · reversed
autodamagesprocedure

In a wrongful death action arising from a truck striking and killing a pedestrian, the trial court granted partial summary judgment to the employer on the estate's claims for negligent entrustment, hiring, training, and supervision because the employer had admitted respondeat superior liability and no punitive damages claim survived. The Supreme Court of Georgia held that the apportionment statute, OCGA section 51-12-33, abrogated the decisional law 'Respondeat Superior Rule,' which had entitled an employer that conceded vicarious liability to summary judgment on direct-negligence claims absent a valid punitive damages claim. The Court reasoned that direct-negligence claims allege the employer's own fault, which the jury must consider under the statute, and that such fault is divisible from the employee's fault. The Court reversed and overruled contrary Court of Appeals decisions.

Why it matters: The decision establishes that an employer's admission of respondeat superior no longer bars a plaintiff's negligent entrustment, hiring, training, supervision, and retention claims, so those direct-negligence claims may proceed to the jury alongside vicarious liability under OCGA section 51-12-33.

The Coastal Bank v. Rawlins
Court of Appeals of Georgia, First Division · decided Oct 29, 2018 · No. A18A1010 · vacated and remanded with direction
proceduredamages

After prevailing on appeal and obtaining a judgment of no liability, defendant moved for attorney fees under Georgia's offer of settlement statute, OCGA Section 9-11-68, based on a rejected $3,000 offer. The trial court denied fees, finding the offer was not made in good faith, but relied exclusively on objective factors such as the offer's relationship to alleged damages. The Court of Appeals held that under the test adopted in Richardson v. Locklyn, a court cannot base a good-faith ruling solely on objective factors but must weigh those factors against the offeror's subjective belief in the strength of its defense, here the no-standing argument. The court vacated and remanded for application of the Richardson test.

Why it matters: The decision clarifies that a good-faith determination under OCGA Section 9-11-68 requires weighing objective factors against the offeror's subjectively reasonable belief, and that exclusive reliance on objective factors is an abuse of discretion.

Stephens v. Castano-Castano
Court of Appeals of Georgia, Third Division · decided May 29, 2018 · No. A18A0100 · affirmed in part, reversed in part
autodamagesexpertprocedure

In an auto collision case, the trial court excluded evidence that the plaintiff's treating physician held a lien on the lawsuit proceeds and barred questioning about an attorney-to-physician referral. The Court of Appeals held that evidence of the physician's lien-based financial interest in the verdict was admissible to show potential bias and affect credibility, and that the collateral source rule did not bar it because the plaintiff still owed the full cost of treatment and no benefit or write-off reduced the obligation. The court affirmed exclusion of the attorney-referral evidence, holding that the referral standing alone was insufficient to show bias and presented a danger of unfair prejudice, and it affirmed denial of the defendant's motion to enforce a purported settlement because the relied-upon email omitted the essential settlement amount. The court noted that any such financial-interest evidence remains subject to a Rule 403 analysis. The opinion is physical precedent only.

Why it matters: The decision clarifies that a treating physician's lien on lawsuit proceeds is admissible to show financial interest and bias and is not barred by the collateral source rule, while a bare attorney-to-physician referral, standing alone, is not.

Cooper Tire & Rubber Company v. Koch
Supreme Court of Georgia · decided Mar 15, 2018 · No. S17G0654 · affirmed
proceduredamages

In a product liability action arising from a fatal single-vehicle crash following a tire tread separation, the defendant tire manufacturer sought dismissal or other sanctions for spoliation after the plaintiff allowed the vehicle and remaining tires to be crushed for scrap. The Supreme Court of Georgia held that the common-law duty to preserve relevant evidence is defined the same for plaintiffs and defendants, arising when a party actually anticipates or reasonably should anticipate litigation, though the practical timing of the duty may differ because plaintiffs generally control whether and when litigation is pursued. The court confirmed that the Phillips factors are a non-exclusive list, that the most severe sanctions are reserved for evidence lost intentionally in bad faith with uncurable prejudice, and that loss through mere negligence normally warrants lesser sanctions or none. Applying a summary judgment standard, the court affirmed the finding that the plaintiff had no duty to preserve at the time the evidence was destroyed.

Why it matters: The decision clarifies that the duty to preserve evidence and the reasonable-foreseeability test apply equally to plaintiffs and defendants, sets out how the Phillips factors and sophistication of the party bear on when that duty is triggered, and confirms that severe spoliation sanctions require intentional bad faith while negligent loss warrants lesser or no sanctions.

Hospital Authority of Valdosta/Lowndes County v. Fender
Court of Appeals of Georgia, First Division · decided Jun 23, 2017 · Nos. A17A0002 & A17A0003 · affirmed in part, reversed in part, and remanded in a17a0002; affirmed in a17a0003
damagesexpertprocedure

In a medical malpractice action, the Court of Appeals held that a fact question existed on whether the new injury exception to the two-year statute of limitation in OCGA section 9-3-71(a) applied, where the patient remained asymptomatic for a period after an alleged misdiagnosis of carotid stenosis and later suffered a massive stroke, so the limitation period could begin on the date of the stroke. The court also held that the Respondeat Superior Rule, which entitles an employer to summary judgment on negligent hiring, training, supervision, and retention claims when it admits vicarious liability and no punitive damages are sought, was not superseded by the apportionment statute, OCGA section 51-12-33(b), reasoning that the statute does not apply to claims based on respondeat superior or to derivative claims that are merely duplicative of it. The court further affirmed denial of a motion under OCGA section 24-7-702(b) to exclude a medical expert's causation opinion. The court reversed summary judgment as to the negligent hiring claims and otherwise affirmed.

Why it matters: The decision clarifies that Georgia's apportionment statute does not displace the Respondeat Superior Rule barring direct negligence claims against an employer that admits vicarious liability and faces no punitive damages claim, and it applies the new injury exception to the medical malpractice limitation period where a misdiagnosed patient remains asymptomatic before a later distinct injury.

Martin v. Six Flags Over Georgia II, L.P.
Supreme Court of Georgia · decided Jun 5, 2017 · No. · affirmed in part and reversed in part, and case remanded with direction
premisesnegligent securitydamages

The Supreme Court of Georgia held that a premises owner can be liable under OCGA section 51-3-1 for a criminal attack that begins on its premises but is completed off-premises, when the injuries are a foreseeable proximate result of the owner's breach of its duty to keep the premises safe, expressly adopting the principle from Wilks v. Piggly Wiggly Southern. The court clarified that the bus stop where the attack concluded was not part of the park's 'approaches,' because the owner had not exercised the control and dominion required to extend its duty to that public way. On apportionment, the court held that an apportionment error under OCGA section 51-12-33 ordinarily requires a retrial limited to the apportionment of fault among tortfeasors, not a full retrial, because the allocation of relative fault is a step distinct from the determinations of liability and total damages. The court affirmed liability, reversed the Court of Appeals' order of a full retrial, and remanded for retrial limited to apportionment.

Why it matters: The decision establishes that off-premises injuries can support landowner liability where the attack originates on the premises and is foreseeable, clarifies the 'approaches' and control-and-dominion analysis, and holds that apportionment error generally requires retrial only on apportionment rather than a full retrial.

Harris v. Mahone
Court of Appeals of Georgia, Fifth Division · decided Mar 1, 2017 · No. A16A1748 · affirmed in part, reversed in part, and remanded with direction
procedureautodamages

In an auto negligence case, plaintiff sought attorney fees under OCGA Section 9-11-68 after recovering a verdict exceeding 125 percent of his pretrial counteroffer. The court held that a counteroffer made within 20 days of trial under subsection (a) must still remain open 30 days under subsection (c), and that fees under subsection (b)(2) run only from the date the offer is deemed rejected through entry of judgment. Because the counteroffer was deemed rejected three days after the trial concluded and plaintiff showed no fees incurred in the window between that date and judgment, no fees were recoverable; a counteroffer can avoid this result only by being withdrawn in writing before its expiration. The court separately reversed the denial of fees under OCGA Section 9-15-14, holding that counsel's statement in his place as to hours and reasonableness on a specific issue was sufficient proof.

Why it matters: The decision clarifies the interplay of OCGA Section 9-11-68 subsections (a), (b)(2), and (c), holding that a near-trial counteroffer must remain open 30 days and that recoverable fees run only from the deemed-rejection date to judgment, and confirms that counsel's in-place statement of hours and reasonableness suffices to support a Section 9-15-14 fee motion.

2016

Richardson v. Locklyn
Court of Appeals of Georgia · decided Nov 15, 2016 · No. A16A0978 · vacated and remanded with direction
proceduredamages

In an auto-collision case where the jury verdict came in below 75 percent of the defendant's rejected settlement offer, the trial court denied the defendant's motion for attorney fees under OCGA section 9-11-68 by finding the offer was not made in good faith based on the offer's relationship to claimed medical expenses and the defendant's stipulation to liability. The Court of Appeals held for the first time that an evidentiary hearing is required before a trial court may rule on a fee request under OCGA section 9-11-68, that a timely objection asserting bad faith triggers the hearing requirement, and that the burden rests on the offeree to prove the absence of good faith. Adopting Florida's framework, the court held that good faith cannot be assessed on objective factors alone but must consider the offeror's subjective reasonable belief, and it vacated the judgment and remanded for a hearing.

Why it matters: This decision establishes that an offeror's good faith under Georgia's offer-of-settlement statute is measured by the offeror's subjective reasonable belief rather than objective factors alone, places the burden on the offeree to show absence of good faith, and requires an evidentiary hearing triggered by a timely bad-faith objection.

Scapa Dryer Fabrics, Inc. v. Knight
Supreme Court of Georgia · decided Jul 5, 2016 · No. S15G1278 · reversed
expertdamages

In an asbestos mesothelioma case, the Supreme Court of Georgia held that an expert's cumulative exposure opinion, which told the jury that any exposure above background, even de minimis, was a contributing cause of the disease, did not 'fit' the legal causation standard and was inadmissible under former OCGA section 24-9-67.1 (b). The Court reaffirmed that, while a plaintiff need not prove a substantial contribution, a de minimis contribution does not satisfy Georgia's 'contributing factor' causation standard, and an expert must tie a cumulative exposure opinion to a reliable estimate of actual exposure showing more than de minimis exposure. Because the expert did not condition his causation opinion on such an estimate, its admission was an abuse of the trial court's gatekeeping discretion. The Court reversed the Court of Appeals' affirmance of the judgment.

Why it matters: The decision clarifies that expert causation testimony in toxic tort cases must satisfy the 'fit' requirement by being tied to a reliable estimate of more than de minimis exposure, and that an 'any exposure' or pure cumulative exposure theory unconnected to actual exposure is inadmissible.

Toyo Tire North America Manufacturing, Inc. v. Davis
Supreme Court of Georgia · decided Jun 6, 2016 · No. S15G1804 · affirmed
damagesexpertprocedure

In a continuing nuisance and trespass action by homeowners against a nearby manufacturing facility, the Supreme Court of Georgia held that a plaintiff who both occupies and owns the affected property may recover separately for past discomfort and annoyance and for the diminution in the property's market value, treating these as two distinct injuries rather than a double recovery, and it expressly overruled Stanfield v. Waste Management of Georgia. The Court also held that alleged deficiencies in an expert appraiser's methodology go to the admissibility of his testimony under OCGA sections 24-7-702 and 24-7-703, an issue committed to the trial court's gatekeeping discretion in the first instance, and not to whether his opinion suffices to defeat summary judgment on causation. Because the defendant had not moved to exclude the expert testimony in the trial court, the appellate courts could consider that testimony in assessing whether causation survived summary judgment. The Court affirmed the denial of summary judgment.

Why it matters: The decision clarifies that past discomfort and annoyance damages and diminution in property value are separately recoverable in continuing nuisance cases and establishes that unchallenged expert testimony with methodological weaknesses may still be considered on summary judgment unless a Daubert exclusion motion is made to the trial court.

2015

Phillips v. Harmon
Supreme Court of Georgia · decided Jun 29, 2015 · Nos. S14G1868 & S14G1893 & S14G1895 · affirmed in part, reversed in part, and remanded
proceduredamages

In a medical malpractice action, the Supreme Court of Georgia clarified that a defendant's duty to preserve evidence is triggered not only by actual or express notice of a claim from the plaintiff but whenever litigation is reasonably foreseeable to the party in control of the evidence, and that constructive notice may be shown by circumstances including the type and extent of injury, the clarity of fault, potential financial exposure, the frequency of litigation in similar circumstances, and the defendant's own conduct such as internal investigations and notification of counsel and insurers. The Court reversed the spoliation portion of the Court of Appeals judgment because the lower courts rested on the legally incorrect premise that actual notice from the plaintiff was required, and disapproved several Court of Appeals decisions to the extent they endorsed that analysis. The Court separately affirmed that the trial judge's ex parte communication with the deadlocked jury, conducted outside the presence of the parties and counsel and never disclosed before the verdict, violated the civil parties' right to be present and, under the unique circumstances including the inability to reconstruct the note, constituted harmful error requiring a new trial.

Why it matters: The decision reframes the trigger for a defendant's evidence-preservation duty around a reasonable-foreseeability standard with enumerated factors, disapproving prior precedent that required actual notice, and confirms that substantive ex parte judge-jury communications in civil trials can require a new trial.
