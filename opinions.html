Allied Property & Casualty Insurance Company v. Bloodworth Wholesale Drugs Inc.
U.S. Court of Appeals for the Eleventh Circuit · decided Jun 11, 2026 · Nos. 24-11398 & 25-11185 · question certified · unpublished, not binding precedent
coveragefirst impression

Two pharmacies sued for their alleged role in the opioid epidemic sought defense and indemnity under liability and druggist liability policies covering damages 'because of bodily injury.' The insurers contended that the underlying government and healthcare suits seek economic losses not tied to identifiable injured persons and are therefore not covered, and the district courts granted summary judgment for the insurers. The Eleventh Circuit found no clear controlling Georgia or Florida precedent on whether such opioid lawsuits seek damages 'because of bodily injury,' distinguishing Georgia decisions like Cesnik, Lunceford, Presidential Hotel, and O'Dell as not dispositive. It certified to the Supreme Court of Georgia and the Supreme Court of Florida the question whether the policies require the insurers to defend and indemnify the insureds in the underlying opioid litigation.

Why it matters: The certification flags an unresolved, high-value coverage question of whether CGL and druggist liability policies covering 'damages because of bodily injury' reach opioid-epidemic suits seeking economic losses, leaving Georgia coverage law on the point to be settled by the Supreme Court of Georgia.

Waddle Trucking of Mississippi, Inc. v. Johnson
Court of Appeals of Georgia, Fourth Division · decided Jun 11, 2026 · No. A26A0145 · reversed
procedureauto

A trucking defendant in a personal injury action was served in state court, after which a codefendant removed the case to federal court, where the defendant filed an answer that was untimely under Fed.R.Civ.P. 81(c)(2). After remand, the state court entered default judgment against the defendant. The Court of Appeals held that the answer filed in district court, even though untimely there, prevented default in state court because the district court never struck the answer and no default was entered under Fed.R.Civ.P. 55, and because on remand the defendant still had time remaining to answer.

Why it matters: The decision clarifies that an answer filed in federal court after removal continues to have effect on remand and prevents entry of default in state court so long as the federal court did not strike it or enter default, even if the federal answer was untimely.

Keathley v. Buddy Ayers Construction, Inc.
Supreme Court of the United States · decided Jun 11, 2026 · No. 25-6 · vacated and remanded
procedureauto

A debtor in an open Chapter 13 bankruptcy failed to disclose a personal-injury claim arising from a car accident, and the lower courts applied judicial estoppel to bar the tort suit using the Fifth Circuit's two-factor test, asking only whether the debtor knew the underlying facts and had a potential motive to conceal. The Supreme Court, assuming without deciding that judicial estoppel can apply in the bankruptcy context and that inadvertence or mistake can be an exception, held that courts must look to the totality of the circumstances surrounding the omission. The Court found the Fifth Circuit's approach both too rigid, because equity eschews mechanical rules, and too broad, because its motive criterion is almost always satisfied, making it a poor fit for assessing whether an omission was actually inadvertent or mistaken.

Why it matters: It establishes that whether a plaintiff-debtor's failure to schedule a personal-injury claim was inadvertent or mistaken for judicial-estoppel purposes turns on the totality of the circumstances rather than a knowledge-plus-motive test, governing how civil litigants raise and defend against judicial estoppel when a tort claim was omitted from bankruptcy schedules.

Hodge v. Molson Coors Beverage Company USA, LLC
U.S. Court of Appeals for the Eleventh Circuit · decided Jun 11, 2026 · No. 25-12983 · vacated in part, affirmed in part, and remanded · unpublished, not binding precedent
auto
auto

A family injured by a semitruck sued a freight broker and a shipper, alleging negligent hiring of the carrier. The district court held that the FAAAA preempted the negligent-selection claim against the broker. Following the Supreme Court's decision in Montgomery v. Caribe Transport II, which abrogated the Eleventh Circuit's prior holding in Aspen American Insurance Co. v. Landstar Ranger, the court vacated and remanded, noting the district court had relied on now-abrogated caselaw.

Why it matters: It applies the Supreme Court's recent holding that the FAAAA does not preempt negligent-selection-of-carrier claims against brokers, abrogating prior Eleventh Circuit precedent that had foreclosed such claims.

auto

The court affirmed dismissal of the negligent-hiring claim against the shipper, applying Maryland law, which requires that the defendant's failure to undertake reasonable inquiry resulted in the contractor's hiring and that the hiring proximately caused the plaintiff's injury. The shipper hired Schneider, who hired Coyote, who hired the carrier, leaving the shipper three steps removed from hiring the operator. Because plaintiffs neither identified authority extending the theory that far nor alleged that the selection of Schneider was negligent, the claim failed, and influence over the hiring process was held not equivalent to actually hiring the carrier.

Why it matters: It clarifies that negligent-hiring liability under Maryland law does not extend to a party several contractual layers removed from the entity that hired the operator absent an allegation tying the harm to the directly hired contractor.

Ya Mon Expeditions, LLC v. YATCO, LLC
U.S. Court of Appeals for the Eleventh Circuit · decided Jun 10, 2026 · No. 25-10140 · affirmed · unpublished, not binding precedent
procedure
procedure

In an interlocutory appeal from the denial of a motion to compel arbitration under the FAA, an MLS company sought to compel nonsignatory yacht-seller plaintiffs to arbitrate antitrust claims based on a subscription agreement their brokers allegedly accepted. The court held that under Florida law, which governs the threshold question of whether an arbitration agreement exists, the party seeking to compel arbitration bears the burden of proving an enforceable agreement, and the movant relied on speculation and assumption rather than evidence that the brokers actually listed the plaintiffs' yachts on its website or accepted the relevant version of the agreement. The court rejected the movant's argument that the nonsignatory plaintiffs opposing arbitration bore the burden of disproving its evidence.

Why it matters: It clarifies that the FAA presumption favoring arbitration does not apply to the threshold question of whether an agreement to arbitrate exists, and that state law places the burden of proving an enforceable arbitration agreement on the party seeking to compel it.

procedure

The court held that the nonsignatory plaintiffs could not be bound to arbitrate under an agency-ratification theory. Applying the Florida Supreme Court's full-knowledge requirement, the court held that a principal ratifies an agent's pre-agency acceptance of a contract only on a showing that the principal had full knowledge of the agreement and its terms, and constructive knowledge does not suffice. Because the movant submitted no evidence that the plaintiffs knew of the agreement or its terms, and the brokers' knowledge could not be imputed because it was acquired before any agency relationship formed, ratification did not apply.

Why it matters: It establishes that under controlling Florida Supreme Court precedent a nonsignatory principal cannot ratify an agent's pre-agency acceptance of an arbitration agreement absent full actual knowledge of the agreement and its terms.

procedure

The court held that equitable estoppel did not bind the nonsignatory plaintiffs to arbitrate. A signatory may enforce an arbitration clause against a nonsignatory under equitable estoppel only if the nonsignatory relies on the agreement to assert its claims and the dispute falls within the clause. Because the plaintiffs' federal antitrust claims arose from statutory duties unrelated to the subscription agreement and did not require reference to or construction of that agreement, the plaintiffs were not claiming the benefits of the contract while avoiding its burdens.

Why it matters: It clarifies that equitable estoppel cannot compel a nonsignatory to arbitrate where the asserted claims rest on independent statutory duties and do not depend on the contract containing the arbitration clause.

Vu v. City of Atlanta
Court of Appeals of Georgia, Third Division · decided Jun 9, 2026 · No. A26A0563 · reversed
autoprocedure

A city employee driving a city-owned truck rear-ended plaintiff's car, and plaintiff served a municipal ante litem notice demanding $1,000,000. The trial court dismissed the suit, accepting the city's argument that the notice failed to state a 'specific amount' under OCGA § 36-33-5(e) because it exceeded the $500,000 sovereign-immunity waiver for negligent motor-vehicle use. The Court of Appeals reversed, holding that OCGA § 36-92-2 does not cap or limit the waiver but automatically waives immunity up to $500,000 while specifying ways to increase it, and reaffirming that the demanded amount need not be one the municipality is capable of accepting.

Why it matters: The decision clarifies that a municipal ante litem notice satisfies the specific-amount requirement of OCGA § 36-33-5(e) even when the demand exceeds the automatic sovereign-immunity waiver under OCGA § 36-92-2.

GoAuto Insurance Company v. 2B Claims Services Inc.
U.S. Court of Appeals for the Eleventh Circuit · decided Jun 8, 2026 · No. 24-13643 · affirmed · unpublished, not binding precedent
bad faithauto

An insurer sued the third-party claims adjuster it hired, alleging the adjuster negligently failed to advise it about the risk of excess bad-faith liability after the insurer let a checks-in-hand policy-limits deadline pass and later settled for about $220,000 above the policy limits. Applying Florida bad-faith law, the Eleventh Circuit affirmed summary judgment for the adjuster, holding that a claims adjuster has no duty to advise an insurer about its own legal risk of excess bad-faith exposure because such advice constitutes legal advice, and that the insurer's own decision to demand signed releases and not deliver the checks by the deadline, not the adjuster's conduct, proximately caused the excess settlement. The court noted the insurer knew of the checks-in-hand requirement eleven days before the deadline and could have delivered the checks.

Why it matters: The decision clarifies that a third-party claims adjuster owes no duty to advise an insurer about its own extracontractual bad-faith exposure and that an insurer's independent settlement and payment decisions, not the adjuster's handling, control causation in a negligence claim against the adjuster.

State Farm Mut. Auto. Ins. Co. & GEICO Indemnity Co. v. Barnor-Cooper
Court of Appeals of Georgia, Fourth Division · decided May 20, 2026 · Nos. A26A0526 & A26A0550 · reversed
coverageauto

The Court of Appeals reversed the denial of summary judgment to State Farm and GEICO in a UM claim arising from a hit-and-run collision, holding the plaintiff was not entitled to UM benefits under either policy. Injured while making a delivery in a Turo-rented Buick, she was not an insured under the State Farm policy: the Buick was not a "temporary substitute car" because the scheduled vehicle it would have replaced had been sold, and the named-insured LLC is a separate legal entity from its members and managers.

Why it matters: Reads the 'temporary substitute car' and named-insured provisions strictly: a borrowed vehicle is not a temporary substitute once the scheduled car it would replace has been sold, and an LLC policyholder is a separate legal entity from its members and managers, which narrows who qualifies as an insured for UM purposes.

Johnson v. Everett
Court of Appeals of Georgia, Fourth Division · decided May 20, 2026 · No. A26A0099 · vacated and remanded in part
procedureexpert

In a medical-malpractice case that ended in a defense verdict, the Court of Appeals held that the trial court mistakenly believed it had no choice but to let the jury consider the emergency-medical-care gross-negligence standard of O.C.G.A. § 51-1-29.5, an issue the parties had not listed in their pretrial order, when in fact the court had discretion to disallow it. The court vacated the denial of a new trial and remanded for the court to exercise that discretion, and found no error in admitting the defense expert's opinions or a complaint from another case.

Why it matters: A pretrial order frames the issues for trial, but the court keeps discretion over an issue the parties left out of it, and treating that discretion as nonexistent is reversible error.

Loveless v. GSH Investments, LLC
Court of Appeals of Georgia, First Division · decided Mar 26, 2026 · No. A26A0113 · affirmed in part, reversed in part
premises

The Court of Appeals reversed summary judgment for an apartment owner whose tenant's visitor was injured when an exterior wooden staircase collapsed, and affirmed the denial of the plaintiff's motions to compel. Before buying the complex the owner received an appraisal and an insurer survey flagging below-average condition and deferred maintenance, yet it never inspected the building exteriors or the stairways and kept no formal inspection process, relying on tenants to report problems. That evidence created a jury question on whether the owner had constructive knowledge of the hazard.

Why it matters: Prior reports flagging below-average condition, combined with the owner's lack of any inspection routine, can supply constructive knowledge of a hazard and create a jury question that defeats summary judgment.

Cannon v. Safeco Insurance Co. of Illinois
Court of Appeals of Georgia, First Division · decided Mar 16, 2026 · No. A25A1847 · reversed
coveragebad faithauto

The Court of Appeals reversed the dismissal of a failure-to-settle action against a liability insurer. After a $1.65 million excess judgment was entered against its insured following a fatal collision, the insured alleged that Safeco had interpleaded and tendered its $100,000 limits into the court registry without obtaining any release of the claims against him. Because an insurer must give equal consideration to the insured's interests and may be liable for an excess judgment for a negligent or bad-faith failure to settle, the complaint stated a claim.

Why it matters: Tendering policy limits by interpleader, without obtaining a release for the insured, does not by itself discharge the insurer or bar a later failure-to-settle or bad-faith claim.

Sullivan v. Trustgard Insurance Co.
Court of Appeals of Georgia, First Division · decided Mar 16, 2026 · No. A25A1720 · reversed
coverageauto

The Court of Appeals reversed partial summary judgment that had set uninsured-motorist limits at $100,000 on a commercial auto policy. The insurer's application directed that its uninsured-motorist selection section was not to be used in Georgia and required a state-specific supplement, and neither that section nor the mandatory offer form was signed or initialed. Because the insurer never obtained a valid written election of reduced limits, the court held the coverage defaulted to the $1 million liability limit.

Why it matters: Without a compliant, signed election of reduced uninsured-motorist limits, the coverage defaults to the bodily-injury liability limit under O.C.G.A. § 33-7-11.

Radheshvar, LLC v. Perez Larios
Court of Appeals of Georgia, Fourth Division · decided Feb 10, 2026 · No. A25A1723 · affirmed
premises

The Court of Appeals affirmed the denial of summary judgment to a motel owner in a premises case brought by a guest's social visitor who was attacked in a breezeway. The owner argued the visitor was a licensee owed only a duty to avoid wilful or wanton injury, but the court held a jury could find he was an invitee, leaving the applicable standard of care for the factfinder.

Why it matters: Invitee-versus-licensee status under O.C.G.A. §§ 51-3-1 and 51-3-2 is often a jury question, which can leave the standard of care for the factfinder and preclude summary judgment.

Giles v. Greenhouse Apartments, LLC
Court of Appeals of Georgia, Fourth Division · decided Feb 6, 2026 · No. A25A2143 · affirmed
negligent securitypremises

The Court of Appeals affirmed summary judgment for an apartment owner and its manager in a negligent-security action by a tenant beaten by unknown assailants on the property in 2020. The landlord conceded it owed the tenant a duty of ordinary care; the affirmance rests solely on causation — the tenant could not establish that any claimed security failure proximately caused the attack — and, given that holding, the court expressly declined to reach the equal-or-superior-knowledge and assumption-of-risk defenses and the challenge to excluded evidence of other crime in the area.

Why it matters: Causation is an independently dispositive element of a negligent-security claim: a defendant can win summary judgment without litigating foreseeability at all. The incident predates SB 68, so the common-law framework applies; for incidents on or after April 21, 2025 the analysis runs through the statutory regime at O.C.G.A. §§ 51-3-50 to 51-3-57.

editor's note Summary corrected 2026-06-11 after re-validation against the opinion: the holding rests on causation alone; the equal-or-superior-knowledge and assumption-of-risk defenses were expressly not reached.

Mejia v. SK Battery America, Inc.
Court of Appeals of Georgia, Second Division · decided Jan 27, 2026 · No. A25A2164 · affirmed
premises

In a wrongful-death action arising from a fatal fall at a battery-plant construction site, the Court of Appeals affirmed summary judgment for the premises defendants on assumption of the risk, analyzed as an affirmative defense. The decedent, a foreman, had actual knowledge of the specific hazard — twelve days earlier he had watched a coworker fall through the same red-taped, non-weight-bearing louvers — and he stepped onto them without tying off and fell over fifty feet; his repeated safety training and written hazard acknowledgment supplied secondary support for the defense.

Why it matters: Actual knowledge drawn from witnessing a prior, identical accident can establish assumption of the risk as a matter of law; documentation like training records and signed acknowledgments corroborates the defense rather than carries it.

editor's note Summary corrected 2026-06-11 after a re-validation flag: assumption of risk is the dispositive affirmative defense, grounded primarily in the decedent's eyewitness knowledge of a prior identical fall.

Venetian Hills Apartments, LLC v. Hughes
Court of Appeals of Georgia, Second Division · decided Oct 31, 2025 · No. A25A1091 · vacated and remanded
premisesnegligent security

In a wrongful-death action after a tenant died in a fire set during an assault by another tenant's guest, the Court of Appeals held the landlord was not entitled to judgment notwithstanding the verdict: it could be liable under O.C.G.A. § 44-7-14 for defective construction and missing fire-safety measures even after parting with possession, and the criminal acts did not demand a defense verdict. The court nonetheless vacated the judgment and ordered a full retrial because the jury charges conflated landlord-tenant and premises-liability law.

Why it matters: A landlord can remain liable under O.C.G.A. § 44-7-14 for defective construction and missing fire-safety measures even after giving up possession, but jury charges that blend § 44-7-14 with § 51-3-1 premises law can require a full retrial.

Williams v. Reddick
Court of Appeals of Georgia, Fourth Division · decided Oct 29, 2025 · No. A25A1226 · reversed
autocoverage

The Court of Appeals reversed summary judgment for a defendant driver in a tractor-trailer collision case. The plaintiff had signed a limited liability release under O.C.G.A. § 33-24-41.1 that preserved claims to the extent other insurance was available, and a fact question remained on whether the employer's policy provided uninsured-motorist coverage, because an undated coverage summary did not establish a valid written rejection under O.C.G.A. § 33-7-11(a)(3).

Why it matters: A limited liability release under O.C.G.A. § 33-24-41.1 does not end the case where other coverage may exist, and a written uninsured-motorist rejection under O.C.G.A. § 33-7-11(a)(3) must actually appear in the record to be effective.

SMG Construction Services, LLC v. Cook
Supreme Court of Georgia · decided Oct 15, 2025 · No. S25G0389 · vacated and remanded
premises

The Supreme Court of Georgia vacated a Court of Appeals decision that had reversed summary judgment for a property owner whose independent contractor fell from an exposed second-story ledge. The Court held the Court of Appeals conflated actual and constructive knowledge and wrongly used constructive-knowledge principles to create a fact question about the contractor's actual knowledge of the open, unguarded ledge. Because the lower court stopped there, it had not addressed all elements of the owner's defenses, so the case was remanded.

Why it matters: Actual knowledge of an open and obvious hazard is analyzed on its own terms under O.C.G.A. § 51-3-1 and is not blurred into the constructive-knowledge inquiry; this is the decision the Mejia panel applied.

Ferriero v. Progressive Premier Insurance Co.
Court of Appeals of Georgia, Fifth Division · decided Sep 10, 2025 · No. A25A0999 · vacated and remanded
procedurecoverageauto

The Court of Appeals vacated the dismissal of a collision suit for failure to serve the defendant. The plaintiff had served Progressive as her uninsured-motorist carrier and pointed to a process server's affidavit and email showing copies left with the defendant's mother, and the trial court abused its discretion in finding no evidence of substitute service under O.C.G.A. § 9-11-4(e)(7) when that evidence was in the record.

Why it matters: Substitute-service proof under O.C.G.A. § 9-11-4(e)(7) that is in the record must be addressed before dismissing for lack of service, and once the limitation period has run the case turns on the plaintiff's diligence in perfecting service.

John Doe v. Jones

Walmart Stores East, LP v. Leverette
Supreme Court of Georgia · decided Jun 24, 2025 · No. S24G1104 · vacated and remanded
damagespremises

The Supreme Court of Georgia vacated a $1 million "nominal damages" award in a premises injury case in which a shopper was struck by a heavy box. The Court held that nominal damages under Georgia law remain only a trivial sum, as at common law, and overruled the Court of Appeals precedent treating nominal damages as having no maximum. Because a million dollars is not a trivial sum, the judgment was vacated, with the remedy and invited-error questions left for the courts below.

Why it matters: Nominal damages under Georgia law remain only a trivial sum, and the Court overruled precedent that had treated them as having no ceiling, so a seven-figure 'nominal' award cannot stand.

Patterson v. United Services Automobile Association
Court of Appeals of Georgia, Fifth Division · decided Jun 20, 2025 · No. A25A0259 · affirmed
coverageauto

The Court of Appeals affirmed the denial of uninsured-motorist coverage to an insured who was struck as a pedestrian and sought UM benefits from USAA. The policy required prompt notice as a condition precedent, and a twenty-one-month delay in notifying the insurer failed that requirement as a matter of law, with the insured's unawareness that UM coverage might apply not excusing the delay.

Why it matters: A prompt-notice condition precedent in a first-party uninsured-motorist policy can be enforced as a matter of law, and a long, unexplained reporting delay, here twenty-one months, can forfeit coverage even if the insured did not realize UM might apply.
