Johnson v. City of Atlanta

// U.S. Court of Appeals for the Eleventh Circuit · decided Jul 12, 2024
Johnson v. City of Atlanta
U.S. Court of Appeals for the Eleventh Circuit · decided Jul 12, 2024 · No. 22-11359 · affirmed
procedure
procedure

Plaintiff sued an officer and the city under 42 U.S.C. section 1983 and Georgia law over injuries sustained during a DUI arrest, and the district court dismissed after considering body camera and dashcam footage not referenced in the complaint. Applying the prior-panel-precedent rule to reconcile conflicting circuit authority, the Eleventh Circuit held that under the incorporation-by-reference doctrine a court may consider a document not attached to or referred to in the complaint when ruling on a Rule 12(b)(6) or 12(c) motion, so long as the document is central to the plaintiff's claims and its authenticity is undisputed. The court found both requirements satisfied by the footage and considered it.

Why it matters: The decision settles a split in Eleventh Circuit precedent by holding that the incorporation-by-reference doctrine has only two requirements, central to the claims and undisputed authenticity, and does not require that the complaint mention the document, a rule that governs what materials may be considered on a motion to dismiss or for judgment on the pleadings.

procedure

The court held that the body camera and dashcam footage established that the officer did not use excessive force when he tackled a noncompliant, resisting arrestee under the totality of the Graham factors, so no Fourth Amendment violation occurred and the officer was entitled to qualified immunity on the federal claims. Because no constitutional violation occurred, the related Monell claim against the city necessarily failed.

Why it matters: The court reaffirmed that where clear video contradicts a complaint's allegations the court accepts the video's depiction, and applied the excessive-force framework to find no constitutional violation.

procedure

Applying Georgia law, the court held that the officer was entitled to official immunity on the state-law claims because the video showed he tackled the arrestee to effect an arrest, not with actual malice or a deliberate intent to injure, as required to overcome official immunity for discretionary acts under the Georgia Constitution.

Why it matters: The court applied Georgia's official-immunity standard requiring a deliberate intention to cause the harm suffered and found it unmet on the video record.
