National Assisted Living Risk Retention Group v. Bishop
District Court of Appeal of Florida · decided Aug 5, 2026 · No. 1D2024-1929 · reversed
coverage

An assisted living resident wandered from the facility in July 2012 and was killed crossing a highway. The facility manager telephoned the insurance agent to report the death and the ensuing state agency investigations, but no demand or suit was made until the estate filed a wrongful death action in 2014. After obtaining a $20 million judgment and an assignment of the insured's rights under the 2012 claims-made policy through proceedings supplementary, the estate sued the insurer for denying coverage and a defense. The First District held that the policy was unambiguously claims-made, that the manager's call was notice of an injury or occurrence rather than a claim as defined by the policy, and that regulatory agency inquiries about whether the facility carried statutorily required insurance were not claims or suits made on behalf of the injured person, so no claim was first made during the 2012 policy period; the final judgment for the estate was reversed.

Why it matters: The decision draws a clear line under a claims-made policy between reporting an occurrence that may later generate a claim and a claim actually being made, and holds that regulatory investigations and inquiries about proof of insurance do not constitute a claim on behalf of the injured person.

coverage

The court separately held that the trial court's finding that the insurer failed to prove a valid retroactive cancellation for nonpayment did not resolve the coverage question. Even if the 2012 policy was never properly cancelled, it expired by its own terms in January 2013, so the claim first made in the 2014 wrongful death action fell outside the policy period and any extended discovery period, none of which the insured purchased despite notices offering it. The court declined to reach reformation or estoppel theories directed at the later policies because the estate had voluntarily dismissed those counts with prejudice and had never obtained an assignment of rights under them.

Why it matters: It clarifies that a defective cancellation does not extend a claims-made policy beyond its expiration date, and that the reporting window turns on the policy period plus any purchased extended discovery period.

City of Brunswick v. Honeywell International, Inc.
U.S. Court of Appeals for the Eleventh Circuit · decided Aug 3, 2026 · No. 23-13200 · motion to dismiss appeal denied; remand order reversed
procedurefirst impression

A city sued two companies in Georgia state court for continuing nuisance and continuing trespass based on pollution around a Superfund site; the defendants removed under the federal officer removal statute, 28 U.S.C. section 1442(a)(1), and the district court remanded. While the appeal of the remand order was pending, the parties litigated in state court and the city amended its complaint twice, and the city argued the appeal was moot. Applying the Griggs principle as explained in Coinbase, the court held that a timely notice of appeal from a remand order reviewable under 28 U.S.C. section 1447(d) automatically stays all district court proceedings, including the remand order itself, so that later state court proceedings are void for purposes of the appeal, and that the earlier mailing of the remand order to the state court does not defeat the stay because the district court retains jurisdiction to reconsider a reviewable remand order. The court denied the city's motion to dismiss and rejected the mootness argument based on the amended state court complaints.

Why it matters: It establishes in the Eleventh Circuit that an appeal from a remand order in a federal officer removal case operates as a self-executing stay of district court proceedings, so post-remand state court activity and amended pleadings do not affect appellate review, a position that diverges from the Ninth Circuit's discretionary-stay approach and aligns with the Fourth Circuit.

procedure

As an alternative ground for denying the motion to dismiss, the court held that the defendants did not waive their right to remove or to appeal the remand order by answering, moving to dismiss, and appearing at a hearing in state court after remand. Waiver principles reach substantial offensive or defensive action taken in state court before removal, and here the only conduct identified occurred after remand and after the plaintiff itself successfully asked the state court to lift its stay pending appeal. Under those circumstances the defendants had no practical choice but to participate.

Why it matters: It clarifies that post-remand participation in state court litigation, particularly where the plaintiff obtained dissolution of a stay pending appeal, does not forfeit the right to appeal a remand order.

procedure

On the merits, the court held that removal under 28 U.S.C. section 1442(a)(1) was proper. The defendants were "acting under" the EPA because, unlike an ordinary regulated party merely complying with detailed regulation, they were carrying out remediation selected in an EPA Record of Decision and consent decree at a listed Superfund site, work the agency would otherwise have had to perform; the city's continuing nuisance and trespass claims premised on inadequate remediation were "for or relating to" those acts; and the defendants raised a colorable federal defense that CERCLA and the consent decree conflict-preempt the state tort remedies. The court also stated that, following the Supreme Court's Plaquemines Parish decision, the "causal connection" and "causal nexus" phrasing previously used for the second prong should be retired in favor of asking whether the challenged acts are closely connected to the performance of federal duties.

Why it matters: It clarifies when parties performing EPA-directed CERCLA cleanup may remove state tort suits to federal court and updates the Eleventh Circuit's articulation of the "for or relating to" prong of the federal officer removal test.

The Town of Pine Hill, Alabama v. 3M Company
U.S. Court of Appeals for the Eleventh Circuit · decided Jul 29, 2026 · No. 25-10746 · vacated and remanded
procedurefirst impression

A municipal water provider sued a chemical manufacturer in Alabama state court on state-law negligence, wantonness, nuisance, and trespass theories for PFAS contamination of its raw water source, and the manufacturer removed under the federal officer removal statute, 28 U.S.C. section 1442(a)(1). The district court remanded, reasoning that the gravamen of the claims was the sale of PFAS products to private paper mills, conduct not undertaken under color of federal office. The Eleventh Circuit held that the first prong of the removal test was satisfied, reading its prior decision that section 1442(a)(1) reaches only current officers as addressed to removal of a state criminal prosecution and not as barring a contractor sued civilly for past conduct performed while assisting a federal superior. The court found the manufacturer's notice of removal sufficiently alleged that it acted under a federal officer in producing firefighting foam to detailed military specifications, and vacated the remand order.

Why it matters: The decision clarifies that a company sued civilly for conduct performed years earlier while assisting a federal officer may still invoke federal officer removal, limiting the reach of the court's prior holding that the statute covers only current officers.

procedure

On the second prong, the court held that the district court should have credited the removing defendant's theory of the case and identified the gravamen of the complaint as overall PFAS contamination of the river rather than only sales to paper mills. Addressing an issue it said it had not previously decided, the court adopted the First Circuit's framework distinguishing express disclaimers that fully eliminate any ground for federal officer removal from artful pleading, and held that a disclaimer requiring a state court to determine the nexus between the charged conduct and federal authority does not defeat removal. It concluded the plaintiff's disclaimer of relief for aqueous film forming foam contamination amounted to artful pleading because commingled contamination from a nearby air base was plausibly alleged.

Why it matters: It establishes in the Eleventh Circuit that a plaintiff's express disclaimer of federally connected claims can sever the causal nexus for federal officer removal only if it wholly removes the federal question from the case, and that a disclaimer leaving a state court to sort out the federal nexus does not.

procedure

On the third prong, the court held that the notice of removal plausibly alleged a colorable government contractor defense under the Boyle elements, pointing to allegations of reasonably precise military specifications, inclusion on a Department of Defense qualified products list, and government knowledge of the relevant hazards. Because the challenge to removal was facial, the court limited its review to the complaint and the notice of removal and declined to consider a multidistrict litigation court's summary judgment ruling on the same defense. It emphasized that the defense need only be plausible and that its ultimate validity is not decided at the removal stage.

Why it matters: It confirms that a facial challenge to a notice of removal is assessed on the complaint and notice alone and that a government contractor defense need only be plausible, not proven, to support federal officer removal.

Michael Davis v. Lockheed Martin Corporation
U.S. Court of Appeals for the Eleventh Circuit · decided Jul 28, 2026 · Nos. 24-10080 & 24-10416 · affirmed
expertprocedure

Plaintiffs in two coordinated toxic-tort cases alleged that volatile organic compounds released from a manufacturing facility caused multiple sclerosis, Parkinson's, and other neurodegenerative diseases, and they offered two general-causation experts. The Eleventh Circuit affirmed exclusion of the first expert, holding that a district court does not abuse its discretion in finding a 'weight of the evidence' causation opinion unreliable where the expert failed to explain how he searched, sorted, and weighed the literature or ranked rival explanations, and separately failed to analyze the Bradford Hill factors in more than a conclusory sentence applied to a single study. Joining the First, Third, Sixth, and Seventh Circuits, the court held that an expert using the weight-of-the-evidence method must explain how he weighed the evidence, and that although the Bradford Hill factors are not always required, a court may require an expert who invokes them to address each in some depth. With no admissible general-causation testimony, summary judgment for the defendant was affirmed.

Why it matters: The decision sets binding Circuit guidance on what a general-causation expert must show to survive Rule 702 review in toxic-tort litigation, specifically that the weight-of-the-evidence methodology must be explained rather than asserted and that a cursory Bradford Hill discussion can support exclusion.

expert

The court separately affirmed exclusion of the plaintiffs' second expert on two independent grounds. His general-causation opinion was unreliable because it merely adopted other experts' reports and 'the science' without explaining any methodology of his own, which the court characterized as the ipse dixit an expert may not substitute for analysis. His second opinion was not a valid background-risk assessment because it rested on a statistician's comparison of mortality rates in a zip code near the facility to rates in more distant areas, rather than comparing disease rates in exposed and unexposed populations, and neither expert knew the actual exposure levels of the compared groups.

Why it matters: It clarifies that an expert cannot establish general causation by wholesale adoption of another expert's report, and it specifies that a background-risk methodology requires a comparison of disease rates between exposed and unexposed populations with known exposure levels, not a geographic proxy based on mortality data.

procedure

Addressing appellate jurisdiction, the court held the district court did not abuse its discretion in certifying under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 54(b) a partial summary judgment that resolved all claims of a subset of plaintiffs in a multi-plaintiff case. The judgment was final as to those plaintiffs even though other plaintiffs' claims remained, and those remaining claims involved different illnesses unrelated to the general-causation evidence at issue. The court found no just reason for delay because the certified appeal raised the same issues, on a materially identical order, as a companion appeal, so requiring the plaintiffs to wait would force two identical appeals.

Why it matters: It illustrates the standards for Rule 54(b) certification in multi-plaintiff mass-tort litigation, including that separability of the remaining claims and avoidance of duplicative appeals can support immediate review of a partial summary judgment.

Patel v. Brim
Court of Appeals of Georgia, Fourth Division · decided Jul 27, 2026 · No. A26A1087 · affirmed
premises

Plaintiffs sued a farm owner for negligence and under the Georgia Dram Shop Act after an underage guest became intoxicated at a graduation party on the property and then crashed a vehicle off the premises, injuring a passenger. Plaintiffs conceded the owner did not sell, furnish, or serve the alcohol, which underage attendees brought themselves, but argued liability should attach because he knew or should have known of the underage drinking and took no steps to prevent it or to stop intoxicated guests from driving. The Court of Appeals held that OCGA 51-1-40 is the exclusive remedy for claims against a third person arising from an intoxicated person's acts, that subsection (b) reaches only one who furnishes the alcohol itself and not one who merely furnishes the premises where alcohol is consumed, and that because the Act is in derogation of the common law it cannot be extended beyond its plain terms. The trial court's dismissal under OCGA 9-11-12(b)(6) was affirmed.

Why it matters: The decision reaffirms that a premises owner who does not sell, furnish, or serve alcohol has no exposure under the Dram Shop Act, and that the Act displaces common-law theories such as failure to supervise minors or failure to prevent an intoxicated guest from driving.

premises

The court separately rejected the argument that OCGA 51-1-40(d), which shields an owner, lessee, or lawful occupant from liability when alcohol is consumed on the premises in the owner's absence and without consent, implies a cause of action where the owner impliedly consented to the consumption. Applying plain-meaning construction, the court held that subsection (d) is clear in insulating owners in the described circumstances and is otherwise silent, and that reading contrapositive clauses into it would render meaningless subsection (a)'s declaration that consumption, not furnishing, is the proximate cause of injury except as provided in subsection (b). The court concluded that the legislature could have created an implied-consent exception in subsection (b) and did not.

Why it matters: The opinion establishes that subsection (d) is purely an immunity provision and cannot be inverted to create liability based on a property owner's alleged implied consent to drinking on the premises.

SFR Services, LLC, Wild Oak Bay Owners Association, Inc. v. American Coastal Insurance Company
District Court of Appeal of Florida · decided Jul 22, 2026 · No. 2D2024-1947 · affirmed in part, reversed in part, and remanded; conflict certified
coverageprocedureexpert

In a hurricane property claim reported about 1,053 days after landfall, the Second District construed the policy's Duties in the Event of Loss or Damage provision, which stated the insurer has no duty to provide coverage if the failure to comply with the listed duties is prejudicial to it, and held that this language places the burden on the insurer to affirmatively prove prejudice rather than allowing it the common-law presumption of prejudice for late notice. Because the prompt-notice duty appears as one subparagraph among other listed duties that carry no presumption, all governed by the same prefatory prejudice condition, the court concluded the parties contracted out of the presumption. The court further held that the trial court improperly weighed the insured's expert engineer's opinion, which was based on a streamed roof inspection, weather data, and pressure calculations, and that the expert evidence created a genuine dispute on prejudice in any event, even under a presumption. Summary judgment on prejudice was reversed and conflict was certified with the Third District's decision in Arce.

Why it matters: The decision holds that policy language conditioning denial on prejudice displaces the common-law presumption of prejudice for late notice, and it certifies conflict with a contrary Third District decision, setting up potential Florida Supreme Court review of which party bears the prejudice burden.

Christie v. Publix Super Markets, Inc.
District Court of Appeal of Florida · decided Jul 17, 2026 · No. 5D2025-1184 · affirmed in part, reversed in part, and remanded
premises

In a slip and fall case governed by section 768.0755, Florida Statutes, plaintiff slipped in a large puddle of water in the water and soda aisle. The court held that evidence of the puddle's size, described as about four feet by four feet, combined with testimony of a leaking float and water drops down the aisle and the presence of a store employee in the immediate vicinity, created a genuine dispute of material fact on constructive knowledge. The court reversed summary judgment on the constructive knowledge claim and affirmed summary judgment on the actual knowledge claim.

Why it matters: The decision reiterates that the size and source of a transitory substance, together with an employee's proximity to the hazard, can supply the circumstantial evidence needed to reach a jury on constructive notice under section 768.0755.

premises

The court affirmed, without further comment, the entry of summary judgment for the store on plaintiff's actual knowledge claim.

Why it matters: The disposition confirms that the actual knowledge theory failed even as the constructive knowledge theory survived.

Annesser Armenteros, PLLC v. Joseph C. Caparo Jr.
District Court of Appeal of Florida · decided Jul 15, 2026 · No. 3D25-2154 · affirmed in part, reversed in part
damagesprocedure

The counterclaimant sought leave to amend to add punitive damages against a law firm and an individual attorney under section 768.72, Florida Statutes. Applying de novo review that tests the sufficiency of the proffer in the light most favorable to the moving party, the court held there was no reasonable evidentiary showing to support punitive damages against the individual attorney, whose name appeared on a quit claim deed only inadvertently and who had no involvement in the underlying representation. The court accordingly reversed the order as to the individual.

Why it matters: The decision applies the section 768.72 proffer standard to reject a punitive damages claim against an individual defendant where the proffer contained no evidence of that defendant's personal involvement in the alleged misconduct.

damagesprocedure

The court held that the proffer and record contained sufficient allegations to allow amendment for a punitive damages claim against the law firm. Because a corporation cannot act on its own, direct corporate punitive liability requires a showing of willful and malicious conduct by a managing agent, and the alleged conduct of the firm's managing partner satisfied that standard. The court affirmed the order as to the firm.

Why it matters: The decision confirms that corporate punitive liability under section 768.72 requires a proffer of willful and malicious conduct by a managing agent, and applies that standard to permit amendment against the entity.

Keais v. Kelley
Court of Appeals of Georgia, Second Division · decided Jul 15, 2026 · Nos. A25A2193 & A26A0122 · reversed in case no. a25a2193; affirmed in part and reversed in part in case no. a26a0122
autodamagesprocedure

A cyclist was struck and killed by a driver who fled the scene and instead summoned a non-driver, an attorney, who observed the scene and made informal calls rather than contacting emergency services. The trial court dismissed the wrongful death claim against the non-driver on the ground that the render-aid and reporting duties of OCGA 40-6-270 apply only to the driver. The Court of Appeals agreed that the statutory duties in OCGA 40-6-270 bind only the driver of the vehicle, but held that a non-driver who voluntarily undertakes those duties may be liable under the voluntary undertaking doctrine of Restatement (Second) of Torts Section 324A, extending Carmichael beyond premises and inspection contexts to a non-delegable statutory duty. Because the complaint alleged the non-driver assumed control of the incident and negligently handled reporting, dismissal was improper at the pleading stage.

Why it matters: The decision confirms that OCGA 40-6-270's stop-and-render-aid duties apply only to the driver while holding that a non-driver who voluntarily undertakes those duties can face wrongful death liability under Restatement Section 324A, extending the voluntary undertaking doctrine to statutory motor-vehicle duties.

procedure

The court addressed its own jurisdiction over the plaintiff's appeal from the order granting the non-driver's motion to dismiss, which was not a final judgment because claims against other defendants remained pending and no OCGA 9-11-54(b) certificate was entered. The court held that because it had granted a co-defendant's application for interlocutory review and that appeal was properly before it, the plaintiff's earlier-filed notice of appeal ripened and was reviewable as a cross appeal under OCGA 5-6-38.

Why it matters: It clarifies that an appeal from a non-final dismissal order that would otherwise require interlocutory procedures may be entertained as a cross appeal when a co-party's interlocutory appeal from the same litigation is properly pending.

proceduredamages

The court analyzed whether the renewal action under OCGA 9-2-61 involved substantially the same parties as the original federal action, examining the case captions of the pleadings and the dismissal order. It held that because the district court's dismissal order still listed the plaintiff in both his individual and representative capacities and nothing showed he had been dropped in his individual capacity, the parties were substantially the same and the renewal was valid.

Why it matters: It reaffirms that renewal-action party identity under OCGA 9-2-61 is assessed by reference to the case captions of the pleadings and dismissal order, and that a plaintiff is not treated as dropped absent a record showing of dismissal in that capacity.

damages

The court held that Georgia's Wrongful Death Act establishes a strict standing hierarchy, spouse, then children, then parents, then the estate's administrator, under OCGA 51-4-2, 51-4-4, 51-4-5, and 19-7-1. Because the decedent left no spouse or children, the parent had standing to sue individually under OCGA 51-4-4 and therefore could not also maintain the action as administrator of the estate. The trial court's denial of dismissal as to the administrator capacity was reversed.

Why it matters: It confirms that a parent with individual standing to bring a wrongful death claim cannot simultaneously sue as administrator of the decedent's estate under the Act's exclusive standing hierarchy.

Angel v. White
District Court of Appeal of Florida · decided Jul 10, 2026 · No. 2D2025-0703 · reversed and remanded for a new trial
procedureexpertauto

In a multivehicle rear-end collision action, the trial court declared a mistrial after the jury deadlocked and then granted a directed verdict finding no negligence by one defendant driver, apportioning all liability to the other driver. The Second District held that the two accident-reconstruction experts gave directly conflicting accounts of the sequence of impacts, and that resolving that conflict by crediting the moving party's expert and rejecting the other driver's account as physically impossible was an improper weighing of evidence. Directed verdicts in negligence actions are rarely appropriate, and the evidence and reasonable inferences had to be viewed in the light most favorable to plaintiff. Reversed and remanded for a new trial.

Why it matters: The decision reiterates that conflicting expert reconstruction testimony about the sequence of impacts presents a jury question that cannot be resolved on a directed verdict, even when one expert characterizes the opposing account as contrary to physics.

Publix Supermarkets, Inc. v. Goga
Supreme Court of Florida · decided Jul 9, 2026 · No. SC2024-0669 · quashed and remanded
procedurepremises

The Florida Supreme Court resolved a conflict among the district courts of appeal over the standard for reviewing a trial court's dismissal of an action for fraud on the court. The court rejected the "more stringent" or "narrowed" abuse of discretion standard used by several district courts and held that traditional standards apply: factual findings are reviewed for competent, substantial evidence, legal conclusions on whether conduct amounts to fraud are reviewed de novo, and the choice of sanction is reviewed for ordinary abuse of discretion. Applying that framework to a slip-and-fall plaintiff who repeatedly misrepresented her physical limitations, the court concluded the trial court acted within its discretion in dismissing the entire complaint with prejudice.

Why it matters: The decision establishes the controlling appellate standard of review for fraud-on-the-court dismissals throughout Florida, disaggregating the inquiry into factual, legal, and discretionary components and eliminating the heightened abuse of discretion standard that district courts had applied.

procedurepremises

Applying the newly clarified standards, the court held that competent, substantial evidence supported the trial court's findings that the plaintiff repeatedly lied about her physical limitations, that de novo review confirmed her conduct constituted a fraud that permeated the entire proceeding, and that dismissal of the whole complaint with prejudice, including the claim for medical expenses, was within the trial court's discretion. The court rejected the district court's conclusion that the medical-expense claim could proceed on purely objective evidence.

Why it matters: It applies the clarified framework to reinstate a full dismissal and rejects the notion that a claim resting on ostensibly objective evidence must be preserved when the plaintiff's fraud permeated the action.

Clayton v. Essentia Insurance Company
U.S. Court of Appeals for the Eleventh Circuit · decided Jul 8, 2026 · No. 24-13873 · reversed and remanded
autocoveragefirst impression

An insured injured by an uninsured motorist while riding his motorcycle collected the statutory minimum uninsured motorist coverage under a standard GEICO policy and then sought additional UM benefits under an adjunctive specialty policy covering his antique vehicle. The specialty policy limited UM coverage to the listed antique vehicle and conditioned issuance on the insured's maintaining a separate policy meeting Alabama's minimum requirements. Applying Alabama substantive law under Erie and predicting how the Alabama Supreme Court would rule, the Eleventh Circuit held that Ala. Code section 32-7-22(j) allows the portable UM requirement to be satisfied through the combined effect of multiple policies, so the specialty policy's UM limitation is enforceable where the insured in fact maintains other insurance providing the statutory minimum. The court reversed the $25,000 judgment against the specialty insurer.

Why it matters: It predicts that Alabama law permits an adjunctive specialty auto policy to exclude portable UM/UIM coverage when the insured separately maintains a standard policy that independently satisfies the state minimum, clarifying how the statutory portability mandate operates across multiple policies.

Trace Elements, Inc. v. MacKensen
Supreme Court of Florida · decided Jul 2, 2026 · No. SC2024-1274 · decision of the fourth district quashed and remanded; second district decision approved
proceduredamages

Two married plaintiffs served an unapportioned proposal for settlement offering to accept a lump sum of $10,000, then sought attorney's fees under section 768.79 after obtaining a larger verdict. Resolving a conflict between the Fourth and Second Districts, the Supreme Court of Florida held that Florida Rule of Civil Procedure 1.442(c)(3)'s command that a joint proposal state the amount and terms attributable to each party applies even to a joint proposal addressing a unified, single claim, and that no exception exists based on the parties' relationship, theory of liability, or the number or nature of the claims. The court reiterated that a 'joint proposal' means simply a proposal made by or to multiple parties, that strict compliance rather than absence of ambiguity is the test, and that Kuhajda's excusal of noncompliance is limited to rule provisions that do not implement a substantive requirement of section 768.79. The Fourth District's decision was quashed, the Second District's contrary decision was approved, and the cause was remanded; three justices dissented, urging a narrower reading of prior precedent.

Why it matters: The decision sets a bright-line strict-compliance requirement that any settlement proposal made by or to more than one party must apportion amounts and terms, even where the claim and damages are legally undifferentiated, which determines entitlement to fees and costs under section 768.79.

Georgia Kenworth, LLC v. Smith
Court of Appeals of Georgia, Second Division · decided Jun 29, 2026 · No. A26A0645 · reversed
procedure

A truck owner sued a repair dealership for negligence, inadequate security, and failure to safekeep his dump truck after it was stolen while on the dealership's premises following completed repairs. The dealership moved to compel arbitration under a clause in the signed repair-authorization order covering any controversy or claim arising out of or relating to the order. The Court of Appeals held that even if the parties formed a separate implied bailment agreement, that bailment itself arose out of and related to the repair order, because the dealership only possessed the truck due to the repair order and the incorporated terms expressly contemplated theft claims. Applying the principle that only a slight causal connection is needed and that doubts about arbitrability are resolved in favor of arbitration, the court found the claims covered.

Why it matters: The decision clarifies that tort and bailment claims tied to the relationship created by a contract fall within a broad arbitration clause covering disputes arising out of or relating to that contract, requiring only a slight causal connection rather than proximate cause.

McGovern v. Rowland
Court of Appeals of Georgia, Fifth Division · decided Jun 29, 2026 · Nos. A26A0116 & A26A0117 · affirmed
autocoverageproceduredamages

After a motor-vehicle collision, the injured party's counsel sent a pre-suit policy-limits settlement offer under the 2021 version of OCGA section 9-11-67.1 that set out the statutory material terms and added non-statutory conditions, including that any settlement check be made payable to counsel in trust for the claimant without variance. The insurer accepted the demand without limitation but issued a check naming an additional payee and requiring endorsement by all payees. The court held that by accepting the offer without limitation the insurer agreed to be bound by the non-statutory conditions as well as the statutory material terms, and that its non-conforming check constituted a rejection rather than acceptance, so no binding settlement formed. The trial court's denial of the motion to enforce the settlement was affirmed.

Why it matters: The decision clarifies that under the 2021 version of OCGA section 9-11-67.1 an insurer that accepts a settlement demand without limitation, rather than expressly accepting only the statutory material terms, is bound by the offer's additional non-statutory conditions, and a check inconsistent with those conditions is a rejection.

proceduredamages

On cross-appeal, the claimant argued the trial court erred by failing to enter judgment on the verdict nunc pro tunc to the verdict date, which cost him about four months of post-judgment interest. The court held that a trial court has discretion, not a duty, to enter judgment nunc pro tunc to the date a verdict is rendered, and that no time limit requires entry of judgment by any particular date. Finding no abuse of discretion, the court affirmed and disapproved City of Milton v. Chang to the extent it stated that Taylor v. Devereux Foundation dictates that a trial court must enter judgment nunc pro tunc to the verdict date.

Why it matters: The decision establishes that entry of judgment nunc pro tunc to the verdict date is discretionary rather than mandatory, which directly affects the accrual of post-judgment interest under OCGA section 7-4-12, and disapproves contrary language in Chang.

Sangphim v. Warcup
Court of Appeals of Georgia, Second Division · decided Jun 26, 2026 · No. A26A0739 · affirmed
procedureautodamagestort reform

In an auto negligence case where the jury awarded the plaintiff $250,000, the trial court awarded the plaintiff attorney fees and expenses under OCGA § 9-11-68 after the plaintiff beat her rejected settlement offer. The Court of Appeals affirmed, holding that whether a settlement offer was made in good faith is determined solely by the offeror's own subjective motivations and beliefs, and the burden rests on the offeree to show that the offer was not made in good faith. The court found the offeree failed to carry that burden where she pointed only to a modest discrepancy between alleged and proven medical damages and an unspecified denial of medical records.

Why it matters: The decision reaffirms that the good-faith inquiry under the offer-of-settlement statute turns on the offeror's subjective beliefs and places the burden on the offeree to disprove good faith.

proceduredamages

The court held that a contingency fee agreement may be considered as evidence of usual and customary fees, but an award cannot rest on the contingency agreement alone; the party seeking fees must also introduce evidence of hours, rates, or some other indication of the value of services rendered. The award was properly supported here because counsel testified about his experience, hourly rate, and the work performed, and submitted detailed work logs, and the court awarded less than the amount requested.

Second, even if there were a genuine interpretive dispute, contra proferentem does not apply where both parties are sophisticated commercial entities with equal bargaining power, and the insured accounting firm qualified as such.

Why it matters: The holding clarifies that contra proferentem is unavailable when a policy is unambiguous and, independently, when the insured is a sophisticated commercial entity, limiting when ambiguities are construed against the insurer.

procedure

The court held that a party may raise canons of construction for the first time on appeal to support an interpretation it advanced below, distinguishing between issues or positions, which can be waived or forfeited through litigation conduct, and authorities or arguments, which cannot. It reasoned that a canon is merely additional authority for the same position and cannot be forfeited any more than the existence of a precedent or the words of a statute. The court also held that a district court's denial of a motion for reconsideration does not change the standard of review applied to the underlying judgment, and that appellate waiver is assessed de novo regardless of the district court's waiver determination.

Why it matters: The decision clarifies that canons of construction are arguments rather than issues for appellate-waiver purposes and that the standard of review on the underlying disposition is unaffected by an unchallenged denial of reconsideration.

Biscayne Beach Club Condominium Association, Inc. v. Westchester Surplus Lines Insurance Company
U.S. Court of Appeals for the Eleventh Circuit · decided Aug 6, 2024 · No. 23-10467 · affirmed
coverageprocedure

An insurer removed a property-insurance coverage dispute, and the parties proceeded to a binding appraisal of the loss under the policy's appraisal clause. On the day the panel met for final negotiations, the insured's appraiser disclosed what he believed was a contingency-fee arrangement, but the insurer did not object and waited until about a month after the roughly $14 million award issued to move to reopen and vacate on partiality grounds. The Eleventh Circuit held that the insurer waived its partiality objection by failing to object at the time it learned of the conflict, applying the arbitration waiver rule that a party who knows of an adjudicator's bias must object before the award issues rather than sit idle to see whether the result is favorable. The court treated insurance appraisal proceedings the same as arbitration for this purpose, reasoning that both involve third-party adjudication of disputes between contracting parties.

Why it matters: The decision establishes that the arbitration waiver doctrine requiring timely objection to an adjudicator's partiality applies equally to insurance appraisal proceedings, so a party that learns of an appraiser's conflict must object during the appraisal or lose the right to challenge it after the award.
