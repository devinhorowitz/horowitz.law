Robertson v. Toyota Motor Corporation
Court of Appeals of Georgia, First Division · decided Sep 1, 2026 · Nos. A26A0988 & A26A0989 · affirmed in case no. a26a0988; affirmed in part and reversed in part in case no. a26a0989
premisesprocedureexpertdamages

A tenant died in his sleep of carbon monoxide poisoning after leaving his keyless-ignition vehicle running in the attached garage below his apartment, and his estate sued the out-of-possession landlord for failing to install a carbon monoxide detector. The Court of Appeals affirmed summary judgment for the landlord, holding that O.C.G.A. section 44-7-14 imposes only a duty arising from defective construction or a failure to keep the premises in repair and does not create a duty of maintenance, and that the absence of a detector from the outset did not show the apartment had deteriorated from its original condition so as to require repair. The court also rejected the estate's implied-warranty-of-habitability theory drawn from Thompson v. Crownover, noting that the duties imposed by O.C.G.A. section 44-7-13 sound in contract rather than tort and that any expansion of an out-of-possession landlord's duty must come from the legislature. It further held that arguments about the condition of the premises before the tenant took possession were not preserved.

Why it matters: The decision reinforces that an out-of-possession landlord's tort exposure under O.C.G.A. section 44-7-14 is confined to defective construction and failure to repair, and that a claim framed as a failure to maintain or as a breach of an implied warranty of habitability does not state a tort claim against such a landlord.

expert

The court affirmed the exclusion of the estate's automotive mechanical engineering expert, who would have opined that the keyless ignition system was defective for lacking an automatic shut-off and that such a feature was technically and economically feasible. Applying O.C.G.A. section 24-7-702(b) and Daubert, the court found no abuse of discretion where the expert did not examine the subject vehicle or an exemplar of the same model year, could not access certain vehicle data, performed no testing or benchmarking, and where the proponent identified no methodology reliably applied to the facts of the case. The court also held that a trial court is not required to make detailed findings when ruling on a motion to exclude expert testimony, because neither O.C.G.A. section 24-7-702 nor case law imposes that requirement.

Why it matters: The ruling illustrates that reliance on general experience with similar fact patterns, without case-specific facts, data, or an articulated and applied methodology, can support exclusion under Rule 702(b), and confirms that trial courts need not enter detailed findings on such motions.

expert

The court affirmed the admission of the estate's human factors expert, who opined on the adequacy of the vehicle's warning system, the applicable standard of care, and a risk ranking derived from a National Safety Council system. It held that the manufacturer failed to explain why the expert's own human-subject testing was necessary where the expert relied on the manufacturer's own human-subject studies, industry standards and guidelines, decibel testing of the warning beeps against ambient engine noise, and other indicia of reliability. The court further held that publication on the specific topic is a relevant but not dispositive consideration and is not a prerequisite to admissibility, and that a party's mere invitation to the trial court to apply an outdated pre-2013 standard is not itself trial court error.

Why it matters: The decision applies the flexible reliability inquiry to a human-factors warnings expert, confirming that a party challenging an expert must explain why particular testing or publication was necessary to reliability rather than simply noting its absence.

damages

The court reversed the denial of the manufacturer's motion for partial summary judgment on punitive damages, holding that punitive damages are not available in a Georgia wrongful death action because allowing both the full value of the life and punitive damages would impose a double penalty. After the estate withdrew its pain and suffering claim, it could not attach punitive damages to remaining claims for funeral and ante mortem expenses, because those expenses grew out of the wrongful death and O.C.G.A. section 51-4-5 operates as a true wrongful death provision rather than a mere survival statute. The court noted that it lacks authority to overturn the controlling Supreme Court precedent the estate asked it to reject.

Why it matters: The holding confirms that punitive damages remain categorically unavailable in a wrongful death action and that pecuniary claims for funeral and ante mortem expenses arising from the death cannot serve as a predicate for them.
